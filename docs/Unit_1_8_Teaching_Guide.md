# Unit 1.8 — Humanity: What Does It Mean to Be Human?

This 20-slide lecture follows the supplied **Unit 1.8.docx** and preserves all eight major sections. Its central understanding is that every human person bears God’s image, has inherent dignity, needs grace and is called to love through responsible choices and community life.

## Pacing and assessment

Allow about **55–65 minutes**: approximately 15 for identity and relationship, 10 for freedom and conscience, 10 for salvation, 10 for vocation and service, and 10–20 for social principles, discussion and assessment. Adjust to the class’s reading pace.

Slide 18 asks students to redesign an inaccessible, expensive class trip using solidarity, subsidiarity and the common good. Its reveal shows a model that respects participation and confidentiality. Slide 19 offers a reflection on identity and one practical action, with a hypothetical alternative. Avoid asking students to disclose disability, finances, personal distress, family circumstances or religious identity.

The exit ticket assesses explanation of dignity, moral reasoning, grace and salvation, and application of a social principle. Evaluate understanding and reasoning rather than a student’s profession of faith. The teaching notes supply suggested answers and discussion follow-ups.

## Teaching clarifications

- Reason and freedom describe human nature, not an ability test for dignity. Body and soul form one person; Baptism adds grace without making unbaptized persons less worthy. See [CCC 355–366](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_6_man.html).
- The note’s “inner voice” description of conscience is refined as a judgment of reason that requires formation. Freedom and knowledge affect responsibility; sincerely feeling certain does not automatically make a judgment correct. See [the Catechism on conscience](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_one/article_6/moral_conscience.html).
- **Abba** means Father and expresses filial trust. Mark 14:36 explicitly uses it; Matthew 6:9 does not print the term. Avoid treating it simply as baby-talk. See [the USCCB note on Mark 14:36](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/14).
- Catholic respect for life includes compassionate care and distinguishes euthanasia from legitimate refusal of disproportionate treatment. See [CCC 2278–2279](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_two/chapter_two/article_5/i_respect_for_human_life.index.html).
- Vocation does not make worth depend on productivity. God can bring good from hardship, but no one must suffer or turn trauma into an achievement to have value.
- The [Universal Declaration of Human Rights](https://www.un.org/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights), proclaimed in 1948, provides a civic connection. It is not presented as a Catholic document or as identical to every country’s law. Rights are not rewards that disappear whenever someone fails a duty.
- Subsidiarity includes support from larger institutions when needed. The common good includes people who would be excluded by a simple majority-preference approach.
