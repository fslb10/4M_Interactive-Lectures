Essential teaching clarifications

Objective means independent of approval, not obvious or easily applied. Natural law is rational participation in moral order, not physical regularity. Scripture and Tradition transmit one deposit; the Magisterium serves it. Distinguish act, intention and circumstances from full personal culpability. Good ends do not justify evil means. Jesus fulfills the law without a caricature of Judaism. The New Law is the Spirit’s grace working through charity. Anger and intrusive thoughts differ from chosen contempt and objectification. Mercy permits protection and boundaries. Salvation is not a points system.

CCC 1965–1974: the New Law

Presenter notes and teaching script

Core scripts appear first in short lessons. Additional notes give depth for discussion; optional material is outside the short core estimate. Students can explain a perspective without disclosing personal beliefs or experiences.

1. Truth & the good

Learning goals: distinguish objective moral truth from preference; explain natural and revealed law; interpret Jesus’ fulfillment of the law; apply moral principles to a concrete choice.

Plan approximately 60–70 minutes. Seven reading pauses occur on slides 6, 9, 11, 12, 13, 15 and 17. Slide 13 contains two short complementary passages. Longer readings can be shared among students.

All four source sections and their reflection questions are mapped in the teaching guide. Classroom slides keep the main ideas brief; presenter notes carry qualifications and examples.

Batch 5: all seven complete original recordings and synchronized captions are embedded. Full classroom plan: 60–70 minutes including discussion.

2. Why this question comes next

Use one or two retrieval responses, not a retelling of every previous lesson. Link image of God, freedom and conscience from 1.8 with faith and reason from 1.10.

The moral life is not a separate project for earning salvation. Connect it to the grace of the Paschal Mystery and new life in Baptism.

The threefold mission gives practical expressions: offer daily work to God, witness truthfully and serve the vulnerable.

3. More than “I like it”

The source contrasts objective and subjective judgments. Define objective as independent of an individual’s or group’s approval, rather than literally located inside a physical object.

Distinguish disagreement about truth from the claim that truth depends entirely on opinion. Disagreement alone neither proves nor disproves moral objectivity.

A descriptive fact, such as ice cream melting, illustrates independence from taste but is not itself a moral claim. Moral claims concern what ought to be done and the good of persons.

Do not caricature everyone who questions moral objectivity as selfish or unable to advocate justice. Present the Catholic account and examine competing reasons respectfully.

4. Goodness rooted in God

The note uses inventor and author analogies. They can express knowledge and care, but moral goodness is not merely whatever a powerful owner commands. Catholic teaching understands God as good, not arbitrary.

Genesis 1:1 is a foundation for a theology of creation, not a self-contained proof of every moral rule. Further reasoning is needed to explain particular moral conclusions.

Human flourishing includes truth, relationships, justice and holiness. It is not identical with pleasure, popularity, wealth or social efficiency.

5. Reason, revelation and faithful teaching

The source calls these three pillars. Treat this as a classroom organizer, not a formal claim that they are three independent sources of revelation.

Scripture and Apostolic Tradition transmit the one deposit of faith. The Magisterium is the Church’s teaching office; it serves the word of God rather than standing above it.

Natural law is accessible to reason, including for people who do not know the Bible. Revelation helps illuminate moral truth and calls Christians into life in Christ.

The Catechism provides a coherent guide to Church teaching. Learning the teaching should include reasons and careful application, not only isolated quotations.

6. Moral awareness beyond a written code

Read Romans 2:14–16. Recall the passage from 1.10, now focusing on its relevance to moral objectivity and responsibility.

Natural law is the rational participation of human beings in God’s moral order. This passage supports the discussion but does not supply every step of a philosophical argument.

People without religious faith can know moral truths and act generously. Moral disagreement should lead to patient examination of reasons and circumstances.

Conscience is a judgment of reason about a concrete act, not an infallible feeling. Its fuller formation is the subject anticipated for 1.12.

Listening discussion: After the recording, invite students to explain what it means for conscience to bear witness. Ask how they would distinguish a moral reason from social approval or fear of punishment. Return to the classroom scenario and identify a reason that could be understood by someone with a different background.

CCC 1954–1960: natural moral law

7. Goods and moral judgment

The note’s natural-law/physical-law analogy needs this distinction. Disease occurs naturally but is not a moral instruction; instincts require judgment.

Universal principles do not require identical practical arrangements everywhere. For example, care for vulnerable people can take different forms according to actual needs.

Ignorance, bias, pressure, self-interest and sin can cloud judgment. Agreement among cultures can invite inquiry, but neither consensus nor disagreement settles whether a principle is true.

Unjust laws and customs can be criticized by reference to human dignity. Laws that permitted slavery did not make enslavement morally right.

Fuller comparison: Recognize goods such as life, truth and community. Apply enduring principles to concrete circumstances. An impulse or natural event does not automatically tell us what is right. A common practice or legal permission is not proof of goodness.

8. The act, the purpose and the circumstances

Explain moral object as the act chosen in its morally relevant description, not merely a physical movement. The same outward movement can have different meanings depending on what is being chosen.

Catholic teaching identifies some acts as intrinsically evil: they cannot be made good by a good intention or favorable consequences. Use the clear example of deliberately condemning an innocent person to gain an advantage.

An objective judgment about an act is distinct from a complete judgment about a person’s culpability. Knowledge, freedom, fear and coercion can affect responsibility.

This prevents two errors: treating every case as a simple slogan, or treating context as permission to make an intrinsically wrong choice good. Do not make claims about a classmate’s state of soul.

9. Freedom with a direction

Read Exodus 20:1–17. The commandments are also found in Deuteronomy 5. Avoid presenting the list apart from the covenant and liberation that introduce it.

Identify worship and reverence, Sabbath, parents, life, fidelity, property, truthful witness and rightly ordered desire. The next slide groups these goods.

The NRSV-CE reads you shall not murder in verse 13. Do not silently replace it with a broader wording and then imply that every taking of life has the same moral classification.

Catholic teaching affirms the enduring moral force of the Decalogue, understood in Christ. The Sabbath command is lived in Christian Sunday worship and rest; it is not simply an unchanged transfer of every Israelite civil detail.

Listening discussion: Have students identify one relationship protected by each commandment they remember. Ask how a prohibition can also defend a positive good. Connect the passage to a present-day example involving honesty, dignity or responsibility, while distinguishing the enduring moral principle from the historical setting of the reading.

10. Boundaries that serve love

The numbers use the Catholic enumeration. The first group concerns God; the remaining commandments concern duties toward neighbor, including inward desires.

Chastity means the integration of sexuality within the person and respect appropriate to one’s state of life. Avoid reducing it to shame about the body; connect bodily dignity from 1.8–1.9.

Respect for property does not cancel generosity or the needs of the poor. Freely sharing bread is not stealing. The source’s bread example is best replaced by this clear distinction.

Honor for parents and respect for authority never require compliance with abuse or an immoral command. Firm moral principles also support protection and truthful reporting.

11. The character of a disciple

Read Matthew 5:1–12. Let students identify humility, mourning, meekness, righteousness, mercy, purity of heart, peace and faithfulness under persecution.

The Beatitudes are not a promise that the faithful will avoid sorrow. Nor do they instruct us to preserve poverty, injustice or abuse. Mercy and justice call for action.

Jesus fulfills the law and forms a people whose lives reflect the kingdom. Do not caricature Jewish moral life as merely external rules without love or inward faithfulness.

The New Law is the grace of the Holy Spirit working through charity, not simply a more demanding checklist.

Listening discussion: Ask students which attitude in the passage challenges the idea that success means power or popularity. Invite them to describe one practical action that expresses that attitude. Discuss why the Christian promise of happiness does not mean that suffering itself is desirable or that injustice should be accepted.

CCC 1965–1974: the Law of the Gospel

12. Let love reach the roots

Read verses 21–24, then announce the move to 27–28. Locate both within the Sermon on the Mount and Jesus’ fulfillment of the law.

Distinguish anger as a feeling from deliberately nurturing contempt, revenge or unjust harm. Just concern about wrongdoing can motivate protection and repair.

Distinguish attraction or an intrusive thought from deliberately cultivating a lustful gaze that treats another person as an object. Avoid framing the body itself as bad.

Reconciliation seeks truth and restored justice. It does not always mean immediate personal contact or renewed trust when safety is at risk.

Listening discussion: Pause after the reading and ask students to trace the connection between an inward attitude and an outward action. Invite them to distinguish an unwanted passing feeling from deliberately nurturing resentment or contempt. Use the distinction to discuss responsibility without suggesting that every difficult emotion is a chosen wrongdoing.

13. The purpose of the law

Read Matthew 22:34–40, then Matthew 7:12. The first is a familiar passage from 1.8 and 1.10; this time ask how it unifies moral obligations.

The Golden Rule is not projecting every personal taste onto someone else. It requires attention to the other person’s dignity, needs and situation.

Love does not mean whatever produces a pleasant feeling. A loving aim cannot justify deliberately choosing an evil means. Return to slide 8 if needed.

Jesus draws on the Torah’s commands to love God and neighbor. His fulfillment should not be described as replacing a supposedly loveless Jewish law.

Listening discussion: Ask students to connect love of God, love of neighbour and the treatment they would hope to receive. Have them test their explanation against a difficult case where kindness requires an honest boundary. Ask what makes the response respectful of both people rather than merely convenient for one person.

14. Grace makes love possible

The source highlights forgiveness and the Lord’s Prayer. Matthew 18:21–22 may be read here if time permits; the NRSV-CE uses seventy-seven times, with a translation alternative. Emphasize abundant forgiveness, not a numerical quota.

Forgiveness does not deny harm, eliminate consequences or require immediate reconciliation and trust. Reporting abuse and maintaining boundaries can be consistent with mercy.

Connect to Baptism and Reconciliation: grace heals and renews. Sin harms communion, but the lesson should leave room for repentance, forgiveness and a new beginning.

Distinguish a moral evaluation from condemning a person’s entire worth. The dignity of both the harmed person and the wrongdoer remains.

CCC 1965–1968: the New Law is the grace of the Holy Spirit, working through charity; external teaching expresses this life. This required qualification now appears in the main explanation.

15. Love must become action

Read Matthew 25:31–46. Ask students to identify both the actions and omissions. Connect this familiar passage from 1.8 to objective duties of charity and judgment.

The Church applies this passage to works of mercy and care for the vulnerable. Avoid reducing the whole account of salvation to a points system for charitable deeds.

Connect to 1.9: moral choices have eternal significance. We do not infer the final destiny of a particular person from one visible act or omission.

Discuss an accessible class response, such as respectful inclusion or a well-planned service activity, while protecting the privacy of people receiving help.

Listening discussion: Invite students to name the concrete needs described in the passage and connect one to a situation they can influence. Ask what the reading adds to a definition of morality concerned only with avoiding harm. Give students time to distinguish helping another person from seeking praise for appearing helpful.

16. Choices become a way of life

This condenses the source’s five benefits: guidance, flourishing, social harmony, character and purpose. Flourishing includes a life directed toward the good, not only outward success.

The athlete analogy illustrates practice and discipline. Like every analogy, it has limits: holiness is not a competitive performance or an achievement that purchases salvation.

Doing right can be costly and does not guarantee popularity, income, safety or an untroubled emotional life. Moral truth is not disproved when a good person suffers.

Nonreligious people can develop virtue and contribute to justice. Explain the Catholic grounding of morality without implying that only Catholics have meaningful lives or reliable character.

17. Hear the word. Put it into practice.

Read Matthew 7:24–27 as the conclusion of the Sermon on the Mount. The comparison is between hearing-and-doing and hearing without doing.

The storm is not proof that someone has sinned or lacked faith. Both builders experience it; faithful living does not remove every hardship.

Connect to 1.10: understanding must become action. Connect to 1.7: ordinary work, truthful witness and service are ways to live the teaching.

Listening discussion: Ask students to explain the difference between hearing a teaching and practising it. Return to their proposed response to the lesson scenario. Invite them to name one realistic action, one obstacle and one source of help, then explain how their choice expresses the moral principle they have identified.

18. “Everyone is doing it”

Give pairs two minutes before revealing. Identify the act as spreading humiliating content, the aim as amusement or approval, and circumstances such as pressure, audience and likely harm.

A claim that everyone consents must be examined rather than assumed. The group’s reaction does not establish the targeted person’s consent or settle moral truth.

Peer pressure may affect responsibility but does not make the humiliation good. Avoid requiring students to discuss actual classmates or display a real image.

Ask how to repair harm if someone already shared it: stop further circulation, correct the wrong, apologize appropriately and support steps to protect the targeted person.

Fuller model retained from the previous revealed response: Forwarding humiliation chooses harm, even if the aim is fitting in. Dignity and truth call for refusing to spread it, supporting the student and seeking appropriate help. Check the facts, avoid gossip, preserve needed evidence responsibly, and work toward protection and repair.

19. A principle, a choice, a habit

This addresses the source’s final reflection and its invitation to consider an admired person’s integrity or legacy. Students can describe a fictional case or a general role model.

Require a principle and an explanation, not only a feeling or a favorable outcome. Include a concrete next step and why the step respects others.

No disclosure of personal wrongdoing is required. Assess moral reasoning and understanding rather than a declaration of religious belief.

If a student has acted wrongly, invite repentance and repair rather than presenting character as permanently fixed. Growth is possible.

Fuller model retained from the previous revealed response: Example: Honest work respects truth and fairness. If friends pressure me to copy answers, I can refuse, ask for legitimate help and offer a study session. Repeating that choice builds integrity while still caring about my friends.

20. Truth that takes shape in love

Model 1: moral truth does not depend on personal or cultural approval. Disagreement and difficult applications do not automatically turn it into preference.

Model 2: natural law is accessible through reason about human goods; Scripture and Apostolic Tradition transmit revelation, and the Magisterium serves its interpretation. Conscience needs formation.

Model 3: Jesus fulfills the law through love, the Beatitudes, inward conversion, mercy and active care. The New Law is lived through the grace of the Spirit, not unaided rule-keeping.

Model 4: specify what is chosen, why, and relevant circumstances. A good goal does not justify an evil means; judging an act does not exhaust a person’s culpability or worth.
