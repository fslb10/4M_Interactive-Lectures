# Unit 1.11 — Catholic Objective Moral Truth

20-slide lecture based on the supplied **Unit 1.11.docx: Catholic Moral Objective Truth**. Allow approximately 60–70 minutes, adjusting discussion to the class. Use an NRSV-CE Bible; the slides give references rather than printing passages.

## Learning goals

Students distinguish objective moral truth from preference, explain natural and revealed law, interpret Jesus’ fulfillment of the commandments, and apply moral principles with attention to the chosen act, intention, circumstances and personal responsibility. The moral life is framed as a response to grace, directed toward love and communion with God.

## Suggested classroom sequence

Ask students to distinguish preference, factual description and moral claim on slide 3. Use slide 7 to test the limits of the gravity analogy. Slide 8 supplies a precise method before biblical moral teaching is applied: what is chosen, why, and in what circumstances.

After Exodus, use slide 10 to identify a protected good and a positive action. After the Beatitudes and Sermon on the Mount, discuss how deliberate attitudes and repeated choices shape behavior. On slide 15, identify sins of omission as well as wrongful actions. On slide 17, note that both builders face storms.

At slide 18, give pairs two minutes before revealing the model response. Require a principle, analysis of the act/intention/circumstances, and active support for the person targeted. Slide 19 is the final reflection: principle, pressure, action and habit. A fictional example or admired role model is welcome; personal disclosure is not required.

The exit ticket on slide 20 assesses definition, sources, Christ’s fulfillment of the law and reasoned application. Model answers are in the presenter notes. Assess understanding and reasoning, not a declaration of personal faith.

## Teaching clarifications

- Objective means independent of personal or cultural approval. It does not mean every case is obvious or every practical response is identical. Disagreement does not itself establish relativism.
- Moral objectivity and an absolute prohibition are related but distinct concepts. Some acts are intrinsically wrong; all moral analysis still requires an accurate description of what is chosen.
- God is understood as good, not as an arbitrary authority whose power makes any command right. The note’s inventor/author analogies need this qualification.
- The note’s “three pillars” is a classroom organizer. Scripture and Apostolic Tradition transmit one deposit of faith; the Magisterium serves its interpretation. Natural law is not a third independent source of revelation.
- Natural law concerns practical reason and human goods. Physical laws describe natural processes; instincts, majority agreement and legal permission do not automatically establish moral rightness.
- Good intentions cannot justify evil means. Circumstances and consequences matter, and knowledge and freedom affect personal responsibility. Judging an act does not determine someone’s whole worth or eternal destiny.
- People without religious faith can recognize moral truth, develop virtue and act for justice. Present Catholic grounding without caricaturing other moral philosophies.
- The commandment grouping uses Catholic numbering. Christian observance of Sunday worship and rest fulfills the Sabbath command in light of Christ. Respect for parents or authority never requires abuse or wrongdoing.
- Generously sharing bread is not stealing. The source’s confusing bread illustration is replaced with the clearer point that property rights do not cancel generosity or duties to the poor.
- Jesus’ fulfillment should not imply that Judaism lacks love or concern for the heart. The Great Commandments themselves draw on the Torah.
- The New Law is the grace of the Spirit working through charity, not only a harder checklist. The source’s examples concerning the heart are treated with the broader CCC 1965–1974 context rather than pinned solely to CCC 1971.
- Unwanted thoughts, attraction and involuntary feelings are not identical to chosen hatred or objectification. Forgiveness does not cancel safety, justice, accountability or necessary boundaries.
- Virtue does not guarantee popularity, prosperity or freedom from suffering. Salvation is not purchased through moral performance; grace calls for a response of faith and love, with repentance and repair when we fail.
- Unresolved bracketed footnote numbers in the supplied note are not reproduced as verified citations. Primary Church sources are linked below.
