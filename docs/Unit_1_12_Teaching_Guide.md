## Source and teaching clarifications

The supplied individual DOCX names Aquinas, Aristotle and Kant. Plato and Levinas are added at the teacher’s request and supported by linked references. The note’s attribution of “aboriginal Vicar of Christ” to Aquinas is corrected to Newman (CCC 1778). Morality and ethics overlap; ethics is not reduced to a professional rulebook. Levinas is identified as a Jewish philosopher. The five questions are teaching paraphrases, not quotations.

### Slide 4 · Romans 2:14–16

**Context**

- Paul is writing to Christians in Rome, where Jewish and non-Jewish believers belong to the same community.
- He explains that God judges fairly. Having received God's law does not make someone automatically good.
- Here Paul describes people who do not have the Jewish law, yet recognize moral responsibilities. Their conscience can accuse or defend their choices.

**Meaning**

- Conscience involves judging a choice: Is this right or wrong? It is more than a feeling of comfort or guilt.
- This supports the slide's distinction: morality concerns our choices; ethics examines the reasons; conscience applies a judgment to a particular choice.
- Catholic teaching calls us to form conscience carefully. Check the facts, listen to sound guidance, and be willing to correct your judgment.

Consider together: Before sharing a rumour, what would you need to know to make a responsible judgment?

### Slide 6 · Galatians 5:13–14, 22–25

**Context**

- Paul is writing to churches in Galatia during a dispute about whether non-Jewish Christians must follow the Jewish law, including circumcision.
- He explains that Christian freedom is a call to serve one another in love, not permission to act selfishly.
- The reading then moves to the 'fruit of the Spirit': qualities such as love, patience, kindness, and self-control.

**Meaning**

- Good character becomes visible in the way we treat people, especially when being kind or patient is difficult.
- Aristotle helps us think about habits and virtue. Paul adds a specifically Christian perspective: life and growth through God's Spirit.
- Practise one small habit, such as pausing before an angry reply. In Catholic life, prayer and grace support this growth; it is not willpower alone.

Consider together: Which quality could you practise in your next difficult conversation?

Background: [Galatians 5 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/galatians/5). Audio and subtitles remain NRSV-CE.

### Slide 9 · Romans 12:1–2

**Context**

- After explaining God's mercy, Paul turns to how Christians should live in response.
- Offering ourselves as a 'living sacrifice' means making our daily lives an offering to God.
- Paul asks believers to resist simply copying the world around them and to let their thinking be renewed.

**Meaning**

- A well-formed conscience takes work. What is popular, normal, or easy is not always what is good.
- The slide connects this renewed thinking with Scripture, Church teaching, reliable facts, prayer, and wise guidance.
- If everyone in a group chat is mocking someone, pause and examine the choice instead of treating the group's approval as proof that it is right.

Consider together: What could help you rethink a choice when your friends all agree with it?

Background: [Romans 12 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/12). Audio and subtitles remain NRSV-CE.

### Slide 11 · Luke 6:27–36

**Context**

- Jesus is teaching his disciples with a large crowd nearby, in what is often called the Sermon on the Plain.
- He asks them to love beyond their usual circle, including people who dislike or mistreat them.
- His model is God's mercy: goodness that does not depend on getting something back.

**Meaning**

- Another person's dignity does not disappear when they are unpopular or difficult to like.
- This connects with the slide's question, inspired by Emmanuel Levinas: What responsibility do I have toward the person in front of me? Levinas was a Jewish thinker; this Gospel offers a Christian connection.
- Include the isolated classmate or refuse to pass on an insult. Mercy can include firm boundaries and seeking help when someone causes harm.

Consider together: What would a merciful response look like without pretending the wrong was acceptable?

Background: [Luke 6 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/6). Audio and subtitles remain NRSV-CE.

### Slide 13 · John 14:15–17

**Context**

- Jesus is speaking to his disciples at their final meal together, before his arrest and death.
- They are facing his departure. He connects love for him with keeping his commandments.
- He promises the Advocate, the Spirit of truth, who will remain with them. They will not have to follow him alone.

**Meaning**

- In Catholic life, morality is a response to God's love and a relationship with Christ. It is more than keeping a list of rules.
- Truth and love belong together: telling the truth should respect people, and caring for someone may require an honest conversation.
- God's grace helps us choose and grow. We do not earn salvation by building a perfect moral score.

Consider together: How could you be both honest and caring when a friend asks you to cover for them?

Background: [John 14 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/john/14). Audio and subtitles remain NRSV-CE.

### Slide 18 · Luke 15:17–24

**Context**

- Jesus tells stories about the lost being found after some religious leaders complain that he welcomes sinners.
- Before this part of the story, a younger son has wasted his inheritance and become hungry and desperate.
- In this reading, he decides to return and admit his wrong. His father runs to welcome him and celebrates his return.

**Meaning**

- Recognizing a wrong choice can be the beginning of change. Failure does not erase a person's dignity or close the door to mercy.
- Catholics see the father's welcome as an image of God's mercy. Repentance and reconciliation are about returning to a relationship, not hiding from God.
- In your own life, take responsibility: name the wrong, apologize, and repair the harm where possible. These are practical ways to respond to forgiveness.

Consider together: What could a real first step toward repair look like after breaking someone's trust?

Background: [Luke 15 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/15). Audio and subtitles remain NRSV-CE.

### Slide 19 · James 1:22–25

**Context**

- The Letter of James gives Christian communities practical guidance about living their faith.
- Just before this passage, readers are urged to listen carefully, be slow to anger, and receive God's word.
- The mirror image describes someone who hears the message but walks away without letting it change anything.

**Meaning**

- Knowing a moral principle is a beginning. Understanding becomes visible when we act on it.
- This brings the lesson together: ethics helps us reason, conscience judges the choice, and our actions put that judgment into practice.
- Choose one action for this week: credit someone's work, include a classmate, or correct a misleading post. Afterwards, reflect on what helped or got in the way.

Consider together: What is one specific good action you can take in the next 24 hours?

Background: [James 1 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/james/1). Audio and subtitles remain NRSV-CE.
