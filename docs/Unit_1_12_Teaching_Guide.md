# Unit 1.12 — Conscience, Morality and Ethics

20-slide lecture based on the supplied **Unit 1.12.docx**. Allow approximately 65–75 minutes, or divide after slide 10. Use an NRSV-CE Bible for the seven core reading pauses; the presentation gives references and listening prompts rather than printing passages.

## Learning goals

Students explain conscience as a judgment of reason requiring formation, distinguish moral principles from ethical reasoning, compare three ethical approaches fairly, and justify a responsible course of action. Christian moral growth is a response to God's grace, expressed in love, virtue, repentance and repair.

## Connections and classroom sequence

Slide 2 retrieves the earlier sequence briefly: creation and the Incarnation (1.1–1.4), Paschal Mystery and Baptism (1.5–1.6), priestly, prophetic and royal mission (1.7), humanity and the soul (1.8–1.9), faith and reason (1.10), and objective moral truth (1.11). Today's distinctive question is how a person forms a sound judgment and acts on it. Retrieve the chosen act, intention and circumstances from 1.11 without reteaching that whole lesson.

Use slides 3–6 to distinguish principles, reasoning, judgment and feelings. Ask how a person could be sincere yet mistaken. On slides 7–10, connect Christian responsibility with grace and growth rather than moral self-sufficiency. Mercy invites conversion; forgiveness and repair belong together.

On slide 13, give pairs two minutes with the lost-wallet case before revealing the response. Require them to identify an outcome, a duty and a virtue. The theories can sometimes agree about an action for different reasons.

On slide 14, distinguish redirecting a threat from using a person's death as a means. Ask what is chosen, intended and foreseen; do not assign a single answer mechanically to every philosopher or treat a thought experiment as a complete account of real emergencies. On slide 15, have students explain why accurately describing urgent need matters before labeling the action theft.

Slide 16 applies reasoning to responsibilities within a role. Presenter notes extend to confidentiality, triage and reporting serious wrongdoing. Ask students to seek facts, appropriate expertise and responsible channels rather than assuming one slogan settles a difficult professional case.

Slide 18 is the main application: a friend asks for help concealing cheating. Before revealing, require a principle, relevant facts, analysis of means and intention, a concrete response and constructive support for the friend. Slide 19 asks for one habit and one action. Students may use a fictional case rather than disclose personal wrongdoing. Slide 20 assesses definitions, formation, ethical comparison and application; model responses are in the notes. Assess understanding and reasoning, not a declaration of personal faith.

## Teaching clarifications

- Conscience is a judgment of reason about a concrete act, not an infallible feeling or every inner voice. Formation includes Scripture, Church teaching, prayer, reflection, reliable facts and wise guidance. Peace alone does not prove an act right; anxiety alone does not prove guilt.
- A person must follow a certain judgment of conscience while continuing to seek truth. Error may be culpable or arise from ignorance for which the person is not responsible. This distinction does not turn objective moral truth into individual preference.
- The note attributes the phrase “aboriginal Vicar of Christ” to Aquinas. **CCC 1778 cites John Henry Newman.** The lecture corrects the attribution. Its discussion of worship and priorities paraphrases the idea without presenting an unverified quotation from Bishop Barron.
- Morality and ethics overlap in ordinary and scholarly usage. The three-part classroom organizer is useful, but morality is not simply private or religious, nor ethics simply public, secular or flexible.
- People without religious faith can recognize moral truth and act virtuously. Consequentialism is not necessarily selfishness: Mill considers everyone's happiness. Kant's principle rejects treating a person **merely** as a means. Virtue ethics includes practical wisdom and deliberate action, not simply following feelings.
- Catholic reasoning attends to consequences but does not justify evil means by good outcomes. In the double-effect discussion, examine the act, intention, whether the bad effect is a means, proportionate reasons and available alternatives. Foreseeing harm does not automatically make an action permissible.
- CCC 2408 qualifies the note's hunger/property example: obvious and urgent necessity to obtain immediate essential needs can mean the taking **does not constitute theft**. This is not a general license to take property whenever someone wants or lacks something. The example shows why the moral description of an act must be accurate.
- Professional duties need sound facts and attention to dignity, safety and legitimate responsibilities. The classroom cases do not promise legal protection for whistleblowing or give clinical instructions for real emergencies.
- Mortal sin requires grave matter, full knowledge and deliberate consent. Do not infer a person's whole responsibility or eternal destiny from a brief case. Repentance, Reconciliation and repair express a response to grace; salvation is not earned through flawless performance.
- Virtue and Christian fulfillment do not guarantee comfort, success or freedom from suffering. Persistent, disproportionate guilt should receive compassionate pastoral and, where appropriate, professional support rather than repeated demands to prove innocence.
