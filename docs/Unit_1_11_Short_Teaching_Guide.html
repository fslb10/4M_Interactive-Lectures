Essential teaching clarifications

Objective means independent of approval, not obvious or easily applied. Natural law is rational participation in moral order, not physical regularity. Scripture and Tradition transmit one deposit; the Magisterium serves it. Distinguish act, intention and circumstances from full personal culpability. Good ends do not justify evil means. Jesus fulfills the law without a caricature of Judaism. The New Law is the Spirit’s grace working through charity. Anger and intrusive thoughts differ from chosen contempt and objectification. Mercy permits protection and boundaries. Salvation is not a points system.

1. Truth & the good

PACING: 0:00–0:32 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: Lesson 1.10 connected faith, reasoning and action. Today we ask whether moral truth depends on what people approve. Catholic teaching says moral truth concerns real human goods and our relation to God. It gives choices a standard beyond popularity. Recall one way that faith and reason can inform a decision; we will apply that partnership to a familiar online situation.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Learning goals: distinguish objective moral truth from preference; explain natural and revealed law; interpret Jesus’ fulfillment of the law; apply moral principles to a concrete choice.

The moral life is not a separate project for earning salvation. Connect it to the grace of the Paschal Mystery and new life in Baptism.

The threefold mission gives practical expressions: offer daily work to God, witness truthfully and serve the vulnerable.

2. More than preference

PACING: 0:32–1:03 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: A preference describes what I like; a moral judgment concerns what ought to be done. Disagreement does not by itself disprove objective truth. Catholic teaching grounds goodness in God, who is good rather than arbitrary. Reason examines human goods; Scripture and Apostolic Tradition transmit revelation; the Magisterium serves its interpretation. These are not three independent sources of revelation.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

The source contrasts objective and subjective judgments. Define objective as independent of an individual’s or group’s approval, rather than literally located inside a physical object.

Distinguish disagreement about truth from the claim that truth depends entirely on opinion. Disagreement alone neither proves nor disproves moral objectivity.

A descriptive fact, such as ice cream melting, illustrates independence from taste but is not itself a moral claim. Moral claims concern what ought to be done and the good of persons.

Do not caricature everyone who questions moral objectivity as selfish or unable to advocate justice. Present the Catholic account and examine competing reasons respectfully.

The note uses inventor and author analogies. They can express knowledge and care, but moral goodness is not merely whatever a powerful owner commands. Catholic teaching understands God as good, not arbitrary.

Genesis 1:1 is a foundation for a theology of creation, not a self-contained proof of every moral rule. Further reasoning is needed to explain particular moral conclusions.

Human flourishing includes truth, relationships, justice and holiness. It is not identical with pleasure, popularity, wealth or social efficiency.

The source calls these three pillars. Treat this as a classroom organizer, not a formal claim that they are three independent sources of revelation.

Scripture and Apostolic Tradition transmit the one deposit of faith. The Magisterium is the Church’s teaching office; it serves the word of God rather than standing above it.

Natural law is accessible to reason, including for people who do not know the Bible. Revelation helps illuminate moral truth and calls Christians into life in Christ.

The Catechism provides a coherent guide to Church teaching. Learning the teaching should include reasons and careful application, not only isolated quotations.

REFERENCE-ONLY EXTENSIONS (no additional recording): Genesis 1:1, 26–27; CCC 1954–1960; CCC 85–86

3. Moral awareness beyond a written code

PACING: 1:03–2:02 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: Paul describes moral awareness beyond possession of the Mosaic law. Natural law means participation through reason in God's moral order, not physical laws or instinct. Life, truth and community are real goods. People without religious faith can recognize them. Principles allow different appropriate applications, while bias and ignorance can cloud judgment. Conscience therefore needs formation, not simply confidence.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Read Romans 2:14–16. Recall the passage from 1.10, now focusing on its relevance to moral objectivity and responsibility.

Natural law is the rational participation of human beings in God’s moral order. This passage supports the discussion but does not supply every step of a philosophical argument.

People without religious faith can know moral truths and act generously. Moral disagreement should lead to patient examination of reasons and circumstances.

Conscience is a judgment of reason about a concrete act, not an infallible feeling. Its fuller formation is the subject anticipated for 1.12.

The note’s natural-law/physical-law analogy needs this distinction. Disease occurs naturally but is not a moral instruction; instincts require judgment.

Universal principles do not require identical practical arrangements everywhere. For example, care for vulnerable people can take different forms according to actual needs.

Ignorance, bias, pressure, self-interest and sin can cloud judgment. Agreement among cultures can invite inquiry, but neither consensus nor disagreement settles whether a principle is true.

Unjust laws and customs can be criticized by reference to human dignity. Laws that permitted slavery did not make enslavement morally right.

4. Act, purpose and circumstances

PACING: 2:02–2:33 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: Examine the chosen act, its intended purpose and its circumstances. A good aim cannot make an intrinsically evil means good: deliberately condemning an innocent person for an advantage remains wrong. Circumstances matter, and knowledge, freedom or coercion affect responsibility. Judging an act does not tell us everything about someone's culpability or worth. Avoid judging a classmate's inner life.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Explain moral object as the act chosen in its morally relevant description, not merely a physical movement. The same outward movement can have different meanings depending on what is being chosen.

Catholic teaching identifies some acts as intrinsically evil: they cannot be made good by a good intention or favorable consequences. Use the clear example of deliberately condemning an innocent person to gain an advantage.

An objective judgment about an act is distinct from a complete judgment about a person’s culpability. Knowledge, freedom, fear and coercion can affect responsibility.

This prevents two errors: treating every case as a simple slogan, or treating context as permission to make an intrinsically wrong choice good. Do not make claims about a classmate’s state of soul.

CORE TEACHING PATH: At Sinai, God recalls Israel's liberation before giving the commandments. They protect worship, life, fidelity, property, truth and rightly ordered desire. Catholic teaching receives their moral force in Christ; it does not transfer every ancient civil arrangement unchanged. Respect for authority never requires accepting abuse or an immoral command. Property rights belong with generosity and attention to human need.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Read Exodus 20:1–17. The commandments are also found in Deuteronomy 5. Avoid presenting the list apart from the covenant and liberation that introduce it.

Identify worship and reverence, Sabbath, parents, life, fidelity, property, truthful witness and rightly ordered desire. The next slide groups these goods.

The NRSV-CE reads you shall not murder in verse 13. Do not silently replace it with a broader wording and then imply that every taking of life has the same moral classification.

Catholic teaching affirms the enduring moral force of the Decalogue, understood in Christ. The Sabbath command is lived in Christian Sunday worship and rest; it is not simply an unchanged transfer of every Israelite civil detail.

The numbers use the Catholic enumeration. The first group concerns God; the remaining commandments concern duties toward neighbor, including inward desires.

Chastity means the integration of sexuality within the person and respect appropriate to one’s state of life. Avoid reducing it to shame about the body; connect bodily dignity from 1.8–1.9.

Respect for property does not cancel generosity or the needs of the poor. Freely sharing bread is not stealing. The source’s bread example is best replaced by this clear distinction.

Honor for parents and respect for authority never require compliance with abuse or an immoral command. Firm moral principles also support protection and truthful reporting.

11. “Everyone is doing it”

Reveal: approval does not make humiliation good. Refuse to circulate it, support the targeted person and seek appropriate help. Pressure can affect responsibility without making the act right. If harm occurred, work toward protection and repair.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Give pairs thirty seconds before revealing. Identify the act as spreading humiliating content, the aim as amusement or approval, and circumstances such as pressure, audience and likely harm.

A claim that everyone consents must be examined rather than assumed. The group’s reaction does not establish the targeted person’s consent or settle moral truth.

Peer pressure may affect responsibility but does not make the humiliation good. Avoid requiring students to discuss actual classmates or display a real image.

Ask how to repair harm if someone already shared it: stop further circulation, correct the wrong, apologize appropriately and support steps to protect the targeted person.

A strong answer respects dignity and recognizes grace rather than treating goodness as unaided rule-keeping. Lesson 1.12 asks how a formed conscience applies this learning to a particular choice.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Model 1: moral truth does not depend on personal or cultural approval. Disagreement and difficult applications do not automatically turn it into preference.

Model 2: natural law is accessible through reason about human goods; Scripture and Apostolic Tradition transmit revelation, and the Magisterium serves its interpretation. Conscience needs formation.

Model 3: Jesus fulfills the law through love, the Beatitudes, inward conversion, mercy and active care. The New Law is lived through the grace of the Spirit, not unaided rule-keeping.

Model 4: specify what is chosen, why, and relevant circumstances. A good goal does not justify an evil means; judging an act does not exhaust a person’s culpability or worth.
