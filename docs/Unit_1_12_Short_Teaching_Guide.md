## Essential teaching clarifications

This short version derives from the approved revised full pilot, preserved unchanged. Teach practical definitions, all five thinkers and extra attention to Aquinas. Morality and ethics overlap; ethics can evaluate rules. Conscience is a judgment, not a feeling, and needs formation. Plato’s ring is a challenge raised by Glaucon; Aristotle’s habits differ from Paul’s life in the Spirit. Aquinas applies knowledge to an act. Kant prohibits treating people merely as means. Levinas is Jewish; Luke is a comparison, not his source text. The Newman phrase in CCC 1778 must not be attributed to Aquinas. Grace and repair matter; hypothetical answers are welcome.

### Slide 2 · Romans 2:14–16

**Context**

- Paul is writing to Christians in Rome, where Jewish and non-Jewish believers belong to the same community.
- He explains that God judges fairly. Having received God's law does not make someone automatically good.
- Here Paul describes people who do not have the Jewish law, yet recognize moral responsibilities. Their conscience can accuse or defend their choices.

**Meaning**

- Conscience judges a particular act; it is more than comfort or guilt.
- The short route links moral principles, ethical reasons and a concrete judgment.
- Check facts, seek sound guidance and correct mistaken judgments.

Discuss: Before sharing a rumour, what must you check?

### Slide 3 · Galatians 5:13–14, 22–25

**Context**

- Paul is writing to churches in Galatia during a dispute about whether non-Jewish Christians must follow the Jewish law, including circumcision.
- He explains that Christian freedom is a call to serve one another in love, not permission to act selfishly.
- The reading then moves to the 'fruit of the Spirit': qualities such as love, patience, kindness, and self-control.

**Meaning**

- Character becomes visible in how we treat people, especially under pressure.
- Compare Plato’s unseen justice and Aristotle’s habits with Paul’s life in the Spirit.

### 1. How do I choose what is right?

PACING: 0:00–0:32 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: Lesson 1.11 asked whether moral truth is more than preference. Today we apply it. Morality concerns right and wrong; ethics examines reasons for judgments; conscience judges a particular act. The terms overlap, so ethics is not simply public rules. In a wallet case, honesty is a moral good, ethics explains the owner's claim, and conscience judges what I should do.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Learning goals: define conscience, morality and ethics; recognize Plato, Aristotle, St. Thomas Aquinas, Immanuel Kant and Emmanuel Levinas; apply a Catholic approach to a familiar decision.

Twenty slides. Keep biographies brief and give Aquinas most attention. The seven existing readings are retained on full slides 4, 6, 9, 11, 13, 18 and 19. Allow discussion according to the class; slide count does not set a teaching duration.

The movement through Unit 1 is knowing God (1.1–1.3), Christ and Christian participation (1.4–1.7), the human person (1.8–1.9), and living responsibly (1.10–1.12). Today turns those ideas into choices.

Model: morality identifies honesty and fairness as goods; ethics explains why deception damages trust; conscience judges that I should not lie here.

Morality and ethics often overlap in ordinary and academic use. These classroom distinctions help us reason; they do not divide personal beliefs from public rules. Ethics also questions whether a rule is just.

Recall Matthew 22:34–40: love of God and neighbor. Conscience belongs to all people, including people with no religious belief.

Use the familiar wallet case throughout. It connects a moral principle, ethical reasoning and a concrete conscience judgment without introducing a crisis.

Do not define ethics as only a professional code or the law. A code may guide action, but ethical reasoning evaluates the code too.

Aristotle (384–322 BC), Plato’s student, connects living well with virtue and practical wisdom. Courage is a fitting response to danger; it is not simply a numerical midpoint or whatever feels comfortable.

The connection with Galatians is a comparison: Aristotle emphasizes habituation; Paul describes life shaped by the Spirit. Christian virtue involves grace as well as practice. Aristotle was not a Christian theologian.

Example: repeatedly doing your share of group work develops reliability. Ask for one repeatable action rather than a vague promise to be a better person.

Plato (c. 428/427–348/347 BC) explores justice in the Republic. Glaucon’s ring-of-Gyges story challenges whether people would remain just if they could act unseen. Do not present Glaucon’s challenge as Plato endorsing dishonesty; Socrates defends the value of justice.

The wallet is our classroom application, not Plato’s original example. Keep the focus on motives and character rather than the theory of Forms. Plato is added at the teacher’s request; he is not named in the available individual DOCX.

### 4. Aquinas: judge and act

PACING: 2:33–3:05 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: Aquinas gives our main Catholic framework. Natural law means rational participation in God's eternal law. We recognize goods such as life, knowledge and community; conscience applies moral knowledge to a particular act. Check both principles and facts. A wallet belongs to someone whose good matters, so arrange its safe return. Reason serves responsible action, rather than merely defending what we want.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), a Dominican theologian, draws on Aristotle while developing a Christian account of human action and our end in God. Natural law means rational participation in God’s eternal law, not merely whatever occurs in nature.

Begin with goods students recognize: life, knowledge, relationships and shared life. From there, explain why lying or cruelty can damage real human goods.

Connect to faith and reason (1.10) and objective moral truth (1.11). Natural law is accessible to human reason; it does not imply only Catholics can act well.

For Aquinas, conscience is the act of applying knowledge to a particular deed. The classroom sequence makes that account concrete.

A mistake can arise from a mistaken principle or mistaken information. Checking facts and learning are part of responsible judgment.

Source correction: the note attributes “aboriginal Vicar of Christ” to Aquinas. The phrase belongs to John Henry Newman, as cited in CCC 1778. Do not repeat the mistaken attribution.

### 6. Kant: duty and respect

PACING: 3:55–4:27 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: Kant asks whether our principle can be universalized and whether we treat people merely as tools. Cooperation is allowed; ignoring another person's dignity and agency is the problem. Compare five questions: Plato asks about unseen justice, Aristotle about habits, Aquinas about applying moral knowledge, Kant about duty, and Levinas about overlooked needs. These thinkers have distinct theories, not identical answers.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Kant (1724–1804) asks about the principle of our action and whether it can be universalized. This is more demanding than asking whether the outcome would be convenient.

People can cooperate and help one another; the problem is treating someone merely as a means, ignoring their dignity and agency. This wording matters.

Kant’s theory is distinct from Catholic moral theology. Compare insights about duty and respect without suggesting that the Church adopts his complete system.

Recall the names through an example rather than requiring dates. Aquinas remains the main Catholic reference. These questions are teaching summaries, not quotations.

Optional extension from the note: consequences also matter. The trolley case can introduce outcomes, duties and character, but avoid making the lesson depend on resolving a tragic hypothetical. No thinker’s position is captured by automatically pulling or refusing a switch.

For a longer discussion, ask what is chosen, what is intended, who is affected and what alternatives exist. Do not suggest that a good outcome permits every means.

[Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Section II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm)

The class example applies that insight without reducing his philosophy to being nice. Responsibility is not earned by a promise of repayment.

### 9. A friend asks you to cover for cheating

PACING: 6:37–7:38 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: A friend admits cheating and asks you to say they worked alone. Take thirty seconds: use Aquinas and one other thinker to judge and act. Then reveal. Refuse to lie, encourage responsibility and offer study support; follow appropriate school procedures. Check facts, notice people, examine the act, seek guidance, judge, then review. The same honesty matters at a part-time job.

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

The friend’s admission is a given fact in the case. Distinguish refusing to lie from deciding how and when to report; school expectations and the facts matter.

Ask students to use Aquinas plus one other thinker. A good answer identifies the act, explains why it matters and offers support without excusing deception.

Model the six steps briefly. Facts: a photo is circulating; do not assume you know the whole story. People: the person pictured and everyone sharing it. Act: forwarding it can increase humiliation. Guidance: dignity, charity and relevant school expectations. Judgment: do not pass it on; seek appropriate support. Review: check whether the harm has stopped.

The process helps conscience judge; it is not a mechanical formula that removes personal responsibility.

Invite an answer before revealing. Accept different sound reasons: Plato—justice without recognition; Aristotle—honesty as habit; Kant—respect rather than using the owner; Levinas—notice the person’s needs; Aquinas—apply moral knowledge.

The correct response needs both a defensible judgment and a feasible way to act.

Retain the note’s professional-ethics connection through a part-time job: a colleague suggests recording hours not worked. Honesty, fairness and trust matter even when there is pressure to fit in.

The note also uses nursing, confidentiality and whistleblowing. Discuss duties in general, not specific clinical or legal instructions. A code is a guide that can itself be evaluated ethically.

Optional food-and-need case: CCC 2408 distinguishes theft from obvious urgent necessity where taking essentials is the only means to meet immediate needs. Do not say hunger always excuses stealing, or that property rights erase essential human needs. This is extension discussion, not the main teaching case.

### 12. Understand the ideas. Use them in life.

PACING: 9:52–10:52 at 125 words/minute, full recording, marked response pause and 3-second transition. Context/Meaning discussion and extension notes add time.

CORE TEACHING PATH: Take thirty seconds: distinguish conscience, morality and ethics, then use Aquinas and another thinker to explain a concrete choice. Our unit moved from God and Christ to dignity, truth and responsibility. Formed conscience brings that learning into action. Carry this question forward: how should our choices and shared institutions protect the dignity and good of others?

The script, complete recording, marked response and transition form the timed core. Source-derived extensions and optional panels add time.

Model: morality concerns right and wrong; ethics examines reasons; conscience judges a particular act. A wallet response can link honesty, the owner’s good and safe return.

Aquinas: apply moral knowledge to the concrete situation. Compare Plato’s justice, Aristotle’s habits, Kant’s duties or Levinas’s response to another. Students need not explain every theory in technical language.

Unit synthesis: knowing God leads to Christ’s saving work and Christian mission; dignity explains why every person matters; faith and reason seek truth; moral truth and a formed conscience guide action. Ask why this learning creates responsibilities in a wider community.
