Essential teaching clarifications

Primary and secondary causes are different explanatory orders, not percentages. Aquinas’s Second Way is not simply a chronological chain or Big Bang argument. Brush, power and instrument analogies have limits for free agents. Physical regularity is not moral law; coercion and ignorance can reduce responsibility. Providence is not fatalism; permission is not approval of evil. Do not blame suffering on a victim’s choices or assign collective Jewish guilt for Jesus’s death. The full lesson retains candle, farmer, study, care and analogy examples; the short path uses plant and study applications.

Slide 4 · Colossians 1:15–17

Context

  • The letter addresses Christians in Colossae. This passage belongs to a hymn praising Christ after thanksgiving for redemption.
  • The hymn places all visible and invisible creation in relation to the Son; the following verses turn to the Church and reconciliation.
  • Our recording stops at verse 17, with creation held together in Christ. Firstborn expresses primacy, not the claim that the Son is a creature.

Meaning

  • Christian creation is more than an event in the distant past: creatures remain dependent on God for existence.
  • This slide connects the sustaining source with Christ. It gives a theological claim, not a replacement for physical explanations.
  • You can study how a plant grows and also receive its existence as a gift. Gratitude should encourage careful attention to creation.

Discuss: What changes when we say creation is sustained, rather than only started?

Presenter notes and teaching script

Core scripts appear first in short lessons. Additional notes give depth for discussion; optional material is outside the short core estimate. Students can explain a perspective without disclosing personal beliefs or experiences.

1. God, creation and real causes

Learning goals: distinguish primary and secondary causality; explain genuine natural causes and human freedom; outline Aquinas’s efficient-cause argument; apply providence without excusing evil or passivity.

Plan approximately 60–70 minutes, allowing discussion. Seven reading pauses occur on slides 4, 6, 8, 11, 12, 16 and 18. A two-part lesson can pause after slide 12.

Students need the central distinction from 1.1, not mastery of every argument. Revisit the meaning of cause before introducing technical terms.

Assess understanding and reasoning rather than personal religious assent. Reflections may use hypothetical examples instead of private experiences.

Batch 1: all seven complete embedded Scripture readings and synchronized captions are available. Context and Meaning are optional discussion panels; opening them pauses audio without losing the position.

2. From God’s existence to God’s action

Ask a student to distinguish cosmic expansion from the philosophical question of why a universe exists. Do not repeat the Big Bang, fine-tuning or DNA material.

Retrieve contingency: dependent beings do not explain their own existence completely. Today moves from that question to the relationship between divine and creaturely action.

Recall Hebrews 1: the Son sustains all things. The new Colossians reading develops this link between creation and Christ.

3. A plant grows: what causes what?

Begin with the visible plant example: light, water, roots and a gardener contribute real causes. The candle and domino examples below are optional extensions.

The candle example comes from the note. Combustion, fuel, oxygen and heat transfer provide physical explanations; a person’s choice also matters. Use an imagined candle rather than requiring a live flame.

A cause is not merely whatever happened earlier. Correlation and temporal sequence alone do not establish causation. Distinguish an actual effect from an unexplained event before assuming a causal story.

The domino analogy illustrates one event influencing another. It does not by itself prove a first member of every possible series or show how God sustains existence.

The statement “every effect has a cause” uses effect to mean something caused. It is not a demonstration that everything, including God, must have an earlier cause.

4. Created through him, held together in him

Read Colossians 1:15–17. The passage praises Christ’s relation to all creation. It supplies a theological claim, not a scientific mechanism.

Explain that firstborn here expresses the Son’s primacy and relation to creation; it does not mean the Son is a creature. Catholic faith confesses the eternal Son as divine.

Connect with the Creed’s profession of the Creator of all things visible and invisible. God does not create and then become unnecessary to creation’s existence.

This develops Hebrews 1 from Lesson 1.1 without reteaching every argument for God. Students should identify the continuing-dependence claim.

5. Primary and secondary causality

Primary means the source of creaturely being and action. Secondary does not mean unreal, unimportant or only apparent. Both natural things and human persons can be secondary causes.

A flame really heats wax. Catholic thought also affirms that the flame, wax, laws and capacities depend on God. God is not proposed as an extra measurable source of heat alongside the flame.

The note calls God Being itself. Introduce this as the classical theological claim that God receives existence from no other, not that God is the sum of all material things.

Avoid saying God is merely in the background: God’s sustaining action concerns the very existence of the acting creature.

Return to the same plant: God’s primary causality sustains being; natural processes and the gardener are real secondary causes. Do not draw God as a competing force or divide causality into percentages.

CCC 306–308

6. In God we live and act

Read Acts 17:24–28. Paul addresses an Athenian audience, speaking of the Creator who gives life and is not confined to a human shrine.

An account of rain involving evaporation, condensation and atmospheric conditions does not become less scientific because a believer gives thanks to God. Belief does not remove the need to investigate those processes.

Likewise, claiming providence does not establish a detailed explanation of why this particular event occurred. Distinguish trust in God’s sustaining care from knowledge of a specific divine intention.

The distinction is a metaphysical and theological account, not a physical measurement that forces everyone to accept it. Invite students to explain the view accurately before evaluating it.

Aquinas: God acts in created agents, I.105.5

7. What does the analogy show—and miss?

Give students a minute before revealing. The analogy helps illustrate genuine mediated action, but it can mislead if a person is imagined as an unconscious object.

The electricity/appliance comparison emphasizes dependence but makes God sound like a physical energy supply. The musician/instrument comparison emphasizes cooperation but cannot fully portray free agents.

An artist, power station or musician is itself a created cause within the world. God’s primary causality is not simply a very large example of such a cause.

The note presents these as explanations, but do not attribute each exact modern analogy or wording directly to Aquinas without a source.

8. Planting and watering really matter

Read 1 Corinthians 3:5–9. Paul is addressing divisions over ministers, not giving a scientific explanation of plant biology. His agricultural metaphor helps us discuss cooperation.

Connect to the source’s farmer illustration. A harvest depends on labor, seeds, soil, light, water and many conditions; no single worker controls them all.

A believer can give thanks to God while crediting workers and studying natural causes. Gratitude should not erase labor, expertise or ecological responsibility.

Use this to resist passivity: if planting and watering matter, trust in God is no reason to neglect practical work.

9. Natural processes and moral guidance

The note’s natural lawful causes primarily means physical and biological processes. Do not confuse this with natural moral law, developed later in 1.11.

Natural laws describe regularities and relationships; they are not ordinarily extra agents pushing things. Fire, living organisms and physical interactions are examples of created causes.

Order does not mean that humans can predict every event. Complex systems and chance at the level of created processes are not automatically excluded by providence.

Catholic affirmation of freedom does not settle every scientific question about determinism. Avoid presenting theology as a replacement for physical research.

10. Our choices make a difference

Explain freedom as the capacity for deliberate action rooted in reason and will. It is not unlimited control or an absence of influences.

The note’s studying example should mean that study contributes to learning and achievement. It does not guarantee success; health, resources, teaching and other conditions can affect results.

Responsibility may be reduced by ignorance, coercion, fear or other psychological and social factors. Avoid the source’s shorthand that every person is 100 percent responsible for everything they do.

Human dignity remains even where a person’s capacity for deliberate choice is limited. Do not equate dignity with independence or achievement.

CCC 1731–1735

11. What we sow shapes what grows

Read Galatians 6:7–10. Include verses 9–10 so the emphasis includes perseverance and doing good to others, not only a warning about consequences.

This is moral and spiritual teaching, not a promise that virtue always brings prosperity or that wrongdoing always receives immediate visible punishment.

Good people suffer, and harmful actions sometimes appear rewarded in the short term. Do not infer a person’s guilt from illness, failure or poverty.

Ask for a small repeated practice, such as truthful work or patient listening. The aim is formation and love, not a technique for controlling every future event.

12. God works in us; we truly respond

Read Philippians 2:12–13 together. The call to work out salvation must not be isolated from the affirmation that God is at work. Fear and trembling conveys seriousness and reverence, not an invitation to obsessive terror.

Catholic teaching rejects both saving ourselves by unaided performance and treating grace as a reason to do nothing. Free cooperation is itself enabled by God’s grace.

Different Catholic theological schools explain the relationship between grace and freedom in different ways. This lesson states the shared affirmations without pretending to settle every technical debate.

Connect with the source’s composer analogy: contribution is real, but the analogy cannot fully explain divine action or make creatures merely follow a forced script.

CCC 1996–2002: grace and a free response

13. From caused action to a first cause

Introduce Thomas Aquinas, 1225–1274, as a medieval philosopher and theologian. The Second Way concerns efficient causation; it is one of the Five Ways, not a summary of all five.

Aquinas argues that nothing can be its own efficient cause, since it would have to be prior to itself in that causal respect. His argument then rejects an infinite regress in the ordered causal series under consideration.

The classroom diagram compresses a complex argument. Ask whether a series of dependent causes requires an underived source, and what an objection would need to address. Do not present the conclusion as established merely by naming it.

The First Cause conclusion requires further philosophical development to establish divine attributes and does not alone establish every Christian doctrine. This builds directly on 1.1.

Aquinas: the Second Way

14. Earlier in time or deeper in dependence?

This corrects the note’s impression that Aquinas merely traces a chronological chain backward until its first event. A traditional reading emphasizes an ordered dependence of causal powers.

An instrument is a limited analogy: adding more instruments does not supply underived agency if each acts only through another in the relevant respect. Students should examine that claim rather than assume all infinite series are impossible.

Aquinas distinguished the world’s temporal beginning, received in faith, from its dependence on a cause. Do not turn the Second Way into an empirical proof of the Big Bang.

The remaining question from 1.1 remains relevant: how does the conclusion connect to the God of Christian revelation? Reasoned theism and the full content of faith are related but distinct.

Aquinas: the beginning of the world

15. Sustained, active and responsible

Magisterium means the Church’s teaching office. The Catechism summarizes the teaching relevant here; the lesson’s slide summaries are paraphrases, not exact quotations.

Contrast occasionalism, which denies genuine created efficient causality, with Catholic affirmation of real secondary causes. Avoid the vague claim that God’s direct action itself is the error.

A deistic clockmaker model pictures creation as left to operate without ongoing divine involvement. Materialism is a different philosophical position, not simply another name for deism.

God can act beyond the ordinary created order; this lesson emphasizes ordinary causality without denying miracles. An unexplained event alone does not demonstrate a miracle.

Aquinas: created agents act

16. Providence is not a promise of ease

Read Romans 8:18–28. This longer pause gives verse 28 its setting: suffering, creation’s groaning, patient hope and the Spirit’s help. Use the NRSV-CE wording rather than the note’s alternate paraphrase.

Providence is not fatalism or a claim that every event is good in itself. The promise concerns God’s saving purpose, not guaranteed comfort, success or a visible explanation for each tragedy.

Catholic teaching holds that chance and contingency at the creaturely level need not lie outside providence. Human inability to predict or explain something does not settle its theological meaning.

Avoid telling a grieving person that a tragedy had to happen or was deserved. Listening, lament, practical care and hope can be more appropriate than a proposed explanation.

17. Permission is not approval

The note’s wording “against His permissive will” is misleading: permission is not the same as positive approval or direct willing of wrongdoing. Do not imply that God causes sin as sin.

Classical theology distinguishes sustaining a creature and its power to act from the defect of a wrongful choice. This does not remove the difficulty of why a good God permits evil; the distinction is part of an answer, not a complete explanation of every case.

Free-will explanations address moral evil but do not by themselves explain all disease, disaster or animal suffering. Catholic teaching also speaks of a creation still on the way toward fulfillment.

God’s ability to bring good out of evil never authorizes us to do evil for a hoped-for good. Prevent harm, protect those at risk and seek justice rather than treating suffering as a task victims must accept.

CCC 309–314

18. God brings life from the Cross

Read Acts 2:22–24, located in Peter’s Pentecost speech. Notice the affirmation of God’s purpose alongside the naming of wrongful human action and the Resurrection.

Do not turn the address to a particular audience into collective blame of Jewish people, then or now. Christian teaching rejects such collective guilt.

Christ’s willing self-gift does not make the injustice of his condemnation good. The saving good comes through his love and God’s victory over death, not through moral approval of his persecutors.

This previews or recalls Lesson 1.5 without repeating that lecture. Causation and providence prepare students to hold human responsibility and God’s saving initiative together.

CCC 597 and 599–618: responsibility and Christ’s saving gift

19. Trusting God while doing our part

Ask pairs to identify the student’s fatalism, then reveal the response. Planning, work, rest and seeking support can all be meaningful actions. They do not guarantee a particular mark.

Extend to the note’s medical example at a general level: a believer may thank God while recognizing the contribution of treatment, caregivers and natural healing. Prayer is not a reason to neglect appropriate practical care.

The source asks about coincidences, answered prayer and things seeming meant to be. Students can reflect gratefully while admitting they cannot prove a particular hidden divine intention from an outcome.

Use a hypothetical example if preferred. Ask students to name what they can do, what is beyond their control, and how they can respond with compassion when results disappoint.

20. Real causes, real responsibility

Model 1: a farmer plants and natural processes produce growth; these are secondary causes. God is understood as sustaining their existence and powers, not as an extra measurable agricultural force.

Model 2: Aquinas examines whether dependent efficient causes require an underived source. His reasoning should not be reduced to counting backward to a first moment in time.

Model 3: God’s sustaining action makes created agency possible. Free wrongdoing remains the creature’s responsibility; divine permission is not moral approval. Responsibility also depends on knowledge and freedom.

Model 4: combine prayer with preparation and appropriate help, recognize limits of control and avoid treating success or failure as a measure of worth or divine favor.

Conclude by returning to Lesson 1.1: the question of the source of being now supports a richer understanding of nature, freedom, responsibility and salvation. Students can raise an unresolved question without being pressured to resolve it immediately.
