Slide 4 · Romans 2:14–16

Context

  • Paul is writing to Christians in Rome, where Jewish and non-Jewish believers belong to the same community.
  • He explains that God judges fairly. Having received God's law does not make someone automatically good.
  • Here Paul describes people who do not have the Jewish law, yet recognize moral responsibilities. Their conscience can accuse or defend their choices.

Meaning

  • Conscience involves judging a choice: Is this right or wrong? It is more than a feeling of comfort or guilt.
  • This supports the slide's distinction: morality concerns our choices; ethics examines the reasons; conscience applies a judgment to a particular choice.
  • Catholic teaching calls us to form conscience carefully. Check the facts, listen to sound guidance, and be willing to correct your judgment.

Consider together: Before sharing a rumour, what would you need to know to make a responsible judgment?

Slide 6 · Galatians 5:13–14, 22–25

Context

  • Paul is writing to churches in Galatia during a dispute about whether non-Jewish Christians must follow the Jewish law, including circumcision.
  • He explains that Christian freedom is a call to serve one another in love, not permission to act selfishly.
  • The reading then moves to the 'fruit of the Spirit': qualities such as love, patience, kindness, and self-control.

Meaning

  • Good character becomes visible in the way we treat people, especially when being kind or patient is difficult.
  • Aristotle helps us think about habits and virtue. Paul adds a specifically Christian perspective: life and growth through God's Spirit.
  • Practise one small habit, such as pausing before an angry reply. In Catholic life, prayer and grace support this growth; it is not willpower alone.

Consider together: Which quality could you practise in your next difficult conversation?

Slide 9 · Romans 12:1–2

Context

  • After explaining God's mercy, Paul turns to how Christians should live in response.
  • Offering ourselves as a 'living sacrifice' means making our daily lives an offering to God.
  • Paul asks believers to resist simply copying the world around them and to let their thinking be renewed.

Meaning

  • A well-formed conscience takes work. What is popular, normal, or easy is not always what is good.
  • The slide connects this renewed thinking with Scripture, Church teaching, reliable facts, prayer, and wise guidance.
  • If everyone in a group chat is mocking someone, pause and examine the choice instead of treating the group's approval as proof that it is right.

Consider together: What could help you rethink a choice when your friends all agree with it?

Slide 11 · Luke 6:27–36

Context

  • Jesus is teaching his disciples with a large crowd nearby, in what is often called the Sermon on the Plain.
  • He asks them to love beyond their usual circle, including people who dislike or mistreat them.
  • His model is God's mercy: goodness that does not depend on getting something back.

Meaning

  • Another person's dignity does not disappear when they are unpopular or difficult to like.
  • This connects with the slide's question, inspired by Emmanuel Levinas: What responsibility do I have toward the person in front of me? Levinas was a Jewish thinker; this Gospel offers a Christian connection.
  • Include the isolated classmate or refuse to pass on an insult. Mercy can include firm boundaries and seeking help when someone causes harm.

Consider together: What would a merciful response look like without pretending the wrong was acceptable?

Slide 13 · John 14:15–17

Context

  • Jesus is speaking to his disciples at their final meal together, before his arrest and death.
  • They are facing his departure. He connects love for him with keeping his commandments.
  • He promises the Advocate, the Spirit of truth, who will remain with them. They will not have to follow him alone.

Meaning

  • In Catholic life, morality is a response to God's love and a relationship with Christ. It is more than keeping a list of rules.
  • Truth and love belong together: telling the truth should respect people, and caring for someone may require an honest conversation.
  • God's grace helps us choose and grow. We do not earn salvation by building a perfect moral score.

Slide 18 · Luke 15:17–24

Context

  • Jesus tells stories about the lost being found after some religious leaders complain that he welcomes sinners.
  • Before this part of the story, a younger son has wasted his inheritance and become hungry and desperate.

Meaning

Presenter notes

1. How do I choosewhat is right?

Learning goals: define conscience, morality and ethics; recognize Plato, Aristotle, St. Thomas Aquinas, Immanuel Kant and Emmanuel Levinas; apply a Catholic approach to a familiar decision.

Twenty slides. Keep biographies brief and give Aquinas most attention. The seven existing readings are retained on slides 4, 6, 9, 11, 13, 18 and 19. Allow discussion according to the class; slide count does not set a teaching duration.

The movement through Unit 1 is knowing God (1.1–1.3), Christ and Christian participation (1.4–1.7), the human person (1.8–1.9), and living responsibly (1.10–1.12). Today turns those ideas into choices.

2. Three words, one everyday choice

Model: morality identifies honesty and fairness as goods; ethics explains why deception damages trust; conscience judges that I should not lie here.

Morality and ethics often overlap in ordinary and academic use. These classroom distinctions help us reason; they do not divide personal beliefs from public rules. Ethics also questions whether a rule is just.

Recall Matthew 22:34–40: love of God and neighbor. Conscience belongs to all people, including people with no religious belief.

CCC 1778–1780: judgment of conscience

3. Morality and ethics: see the difference

Use the familiar wallet case throughout. It connects a moral principle, ethical reasoning and a concrete conscience judgment without introducing a crisis.

Do not define ethics as only a professional code or the law. A code may guide action, but ethical reasoning evaluates the code too.

4. Conscience: a judgment I make

Conscience is a judgment of reason, not simply a mood or fear of punishment. Feelings can alert us to a concern but can also mislead.

Romans 2 presents conscience and moral responsibility beyond one religious community. Explain that people with different beliefs can recognize and do good.

A person must follow a certain judgment of conscience, while continuing to seek truth and correct error. Being sincere does not make every judgment correct.

CCC 1778–1780: judgment of conscience

CCC 1790–1794: erroneous judgment

5. Plato: do right when unseen

Plato (c. 428/427–348/347 BC) explores justice in the Republic. Glaucon’s ring-of-Gyges story challenges whether people would remain just if they could act unseen. Do not present Glaucon’s challenge as Plato endorsing dishonesty; Socrates defends the value of justice.

The wallet is our classroom application, not Plato’s original example. Keep the focus on motives and character rather than the theory of Forms. Plato is added at the teacher’s request; he is not named in the available individual DOCX.

Plato, Republic, Book II: the challenge of the ring of Gyges

6. Aristotle: practice the person you want to be

Aristotle (384–322 BC), Plato’s student, connects living well with virtue and practical wisdom. Courage is a fitting response to danger; it is not simply a numerical midpoint or whatever feels comfortable.

The connection with Galatians is a comparison: Aristotle emphasizes habituation; Paul describes life shaped by the Spirit. Christian virtue involves grace as well as practice. Aristotle was not a Christian theologian.

Example: repeatedly doing your share of group work develops reliability. Ask for one repeatable action rather than a vague promise to be a better person.

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II

7. Aquinas: reason helps us recognize the good

Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), a Dominican theologian, draws on Aristotle while developing a Christian account of human action and our end in God. Natural law means rational participation in God’s eternal law, not merely whatever occurs in nature.

Begin with goods students recognize: life, knowledge, relationships and shared life. From there, explain why lying or cruelty can damage real human goods.

Connect to faith and reason (1.10) and objective moral truth (1.11). Natural law is accessible to human reason; it does not imply only Catholics can act well.

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I–II, q.94, a.2

8. Aquinas: conscience puts knowledge to work

For Aquinas, conscience is the act of applying knowledge to a particular deed. The classroom sequence makes that account concrete.

A mistake can arise from a mistaken principle or mistaken information. Checking facts and learning are part of responsible judgment.

Source correction: the note attributes “aboriginal Vicar of Christ” to Aquinas. The phrase belongs to John Henry Newman, as cited in CCC 1778. Do not repeat the mistaken attribution.

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q.79, a.13

9. Form conscience before the difficult moment

Romans 12 links renewal with discerning the good. Name a concrete routine: pause before forwarding a claim, verify its truth, consider its effects and seek trusted advice.

Formation can challenge what I want to hear. A certain judgment binds, but a person remains responsible for seeking truth; avoid teaching either blind obedience to others or “anything I feel is right.”

CCC 1783–1785: formation of conscience

CCC 1790–1794: erroneous judgment

10. Kant: respect people and keep your duties

Kant (1724–1804) asks about the principle of our action and whether it can be universalized. This is more demanding than asking whether the outcome would be convenient.

People can cooperate and help one another; the problem is treating someone merely as a means, ignoring their dignity and agency. This wording matters.

Kant’s theory is distinct from Catholic moral theology. Compare insights about duty and respect without suggesting that the Church adopts his complete system.

Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Section II

11. Levinas: notice the person who needs you

Emmanuel Levinas (1906–1995) emphasizes responsibility awakened by encountering another person. His “face” is not a judgment based on facial appearance; it expresses the other person’s presence and vulnerability.

The class example applies that insight without reducing his philosophy to being nice. Responsibility is not earned by a promise of repayment.

Luke 6 provides a Christian connection with generous love. Levinas is a Jewish thinker, not a Catholic theologian, and his philosophy is not simply a commentary on this passage. His inclusion is requested by the teacher; he is not named in the available individual DOCX.

Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Emmanuel Levinas

12. Five thinkers, five useful questions

Recall the names through an example rather than requiring dates. Aquinas remains the main Catholic reference. These questions are teaching summaries, not quotations.

Optional extension from the note: consequences also matter. The trolley case can introduce outcomes, duties and character, but avoid making the lesson depend on resolving a tragic hypothetical. No thinker’s position is captured by automatically pulling or refusing a switch.

For a longer discussion, ask what is chosen, what is intended, who is affected and what alternatives exist. Do not suggest that a good outcome permits every means.

13. The Catholic perspective: truth lived in love

Connect dignity (1.8), Christ’s saving work (1.5), Baptism (1.6) and moral truth (1.11). John 14 links love, commandments and the gift of the Spirit.

Catholic moral reasoning considers the chosen act, intention and circumstances. A compassionate intention alone cannot make a dishonest means good. Grace supports responsibility and forgiveness; it is not an ethical score.

Recall Matthew 22:34–40 as the love-of-God-and-neighbor summary. Assess students’ understanding and reasoning, not a personal profession of faith.

14. A practical process for a real choice

Model the six steps briefly. Facts: a photo is circulating; do not assume you know the whole story. People: the person pictured and everyone sharing it. Act: forwarding it can increase humiliation. Guidance: dignity, charity and relevant school expectations. Judgment: do not pass it on; seek appropriate support. Review: check whether the harm has stopped.

The process helps conscience judge; it is not a mechanical formula that removes personal responsibility.

15. The wallet: explain your reasons

Invite an answer before revealing. Accept different sound reasons: Plato—justice without recognition; Aristotle—honesty as habit; Kant—respect rather than using the owner; Levinas—notice the person’s needs; Aquinas—apply moral knowledge.

The correct response needs both a defensible judgment and a feasible way to act.

16. A friend asks you to cover for cheating

The friend’s admission is a given fact in the case. Distinguish refusing to lie from deciding how and when to report; school expectations and the facts matter.

Ask students to use Aquinas plus one other thinker. A good answer identifies the act, explains why it matters and offers support without excusing deception.

17. Ethics beyond class: responsibility in a role

Retain the note’s professional-ethics connection through a part-time job: a colleague suggests recording hours not worked. Honesty, fairness and trust matter even when there is pressure to fit in.

The note also uses nursing, confidentiality and whistleblowing. Discuss duties in general, not specific clinical or legal instructions. A code is a guide that can itself be evaluated ethically.

Optional food-and-need case: CCC 2408 distinguishes theft from obvious urgent necessity where taking essentials is the only means to meet immediate needs. Do not say hunger always excuses stealing, or that property rights erase essential human needs. This is extension discussion, not the main teaching case.

CCC 2408: respect for persons and goods

18. When I get it wrong: return and repair

Luke 15:17–24 depicts the son’s return and the father’s welcome. Connect forgiveness with a real change of direction, not permission to repeat the harm.

Catholics also seek reconciliation with God through the sacrament of Penance. In the example, repair may include correcting the rumor with the people who heard it and apologizing without demanding instant forgiveness.

Students may use a fictional case. Do not ask for public confession or suggest that everyone who feels guilty has done wrong.

19. This week: put one idea into practice

James 1:22–25 challenges hearing without action. Ask for an achievable plan: honest group work, checking a claim before sharing, including a classmate or repairing an unkind comment.

Allow a hypothetical example. Assess the clarity of the reason and action, not the amount of personal information disclosed.

20. Understand the ideas. Use them in life.

Model: morality concerns right and wrong; ethics examines reasons; conscience judges a particular act. A wallet response can link honesty, the owner’s good and safe return.

Aquinas: apply moral knowledge to the concrete situation. Compare Plato’s justice, Aristotle’s habits, Kant’s duties or Levinas’s response to another. Students need not explain every theory in technical language.

Unit synthesis: knowing God leads to Christ’s saving work and Christian mission; dignity explains why every person matters; faith and reason seek truth; moral truth and a formed conscience guide action. Ask why this learning creates responsibilities in a wider community.
