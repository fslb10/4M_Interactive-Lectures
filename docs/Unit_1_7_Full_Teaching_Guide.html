Essential teaching clarifications

Priest, Prophet and King are aspects of the one Christ, not separate persons. His self-offering is unique; the Eucharist makes it sacramentally present without another death. Common and ministerial priesthood differ; Baptism does not confer ordination. A living sacrifice does not mean seeking pain or remaining in abuse. Prophet means witness to divine truth, not merely prediction; the Son reveals the Father as a distinct Person. The Spirit’s guidance does not make personal opinions infallible. Christ’s Kingdom has social consequences and cannot be equated with a party or country. Royal service includes self-mastery, protection and justice. Every person has dignity, regardless of baptismal status. The maintained sequence is Priest, Prophet, King, while the teacher note orders Priest, King, Prophet; every section is retained.

Teaching priorities and assessment

Teach the stated question, the complete spoken core where supplied, and the seven full recordings. Offer the case before its model response; assess accurate explanation and a reasoned action, not personal faith or disclosure. Short lessons include two 30-second responses; lengthen them when the class needs more time.

Slide 4 · Luke 4:16–21

Context

  • Early in his ministry, Jesus returns to Nazareth and reads in the synagogue on the Sabbath.
  • He reads from Isaiah about the Spirit’s anointing, good news and liberation, then addresses those present.
  • The excerpt ends with his announcement of fulfilment. The mixed response and rejection follow later in the chapter.

Meaning

  • Christ and Messiah mean Anointed One: Jesus’ mission is empowered by the Spirit.
  • This opening reading introduces mission; the whole biblical witness develops Priest, Prophet and King.
  • Attention to the poor and oppressed belongs to Christian witness, without reducing salvation to generic helpfulness.

Discuss: What does Jesus’ announcement show about the purpose of his mission?

Presenter notes and teaching script

Core scripts appear first in short lessons. Additional notes give depth for discussion; optional material is outside the short core estimate. Students can explain a perspective without disclosing personal beliefs or experiences.

1. Priest, Prophet and King

The source title lists Prophet, King, Priest; its main sections teach Priest, King, Prophet. This lecture teaches Priest, Prophet, King in the familiar summary order. These are three aspects of the one Jesus, not three persons.

Goals: explain each title with Scripture; connect each to Christ’s saving work; describe how the baptized participate without replacing Jesus or becoming ordained ministers.

Plan 60–70 minutes: 5 retrieval, 40 explanation and readings, 10 application, 5 exit/bridge, with up to 10 extra minutes of discussion.

Seven complete core readings now occur on slides 4, 6, 9, 10, 12, 15 and 16, mapped by reference to the preserved recording IDs. The teacher note orders Priest, King, Prophet; this maintained sequence groups Priest, Prophet, King. All five note sections remain covered.

CCC 783–786: a priestly, prophetic and royal people

2. Why this question comes next

Keep this retrieval bridge brief: one or two remembered connections are enough. Do not reteach all six lessons.

The nature of God and Incarnation explain who Jesus is; the Paschal Mystery explains the saving work; Baptism explains how Christians are joined to him.

The causation connection is limited: God can use created means. It does not mean a person’s natural effort produces saving grace.

In Lesson 1.6, the baptismal anointing and 1 Peter introduced priest, prophet and king. Here we explain these roles in depth.

3. One Person, fully God and human

Define hypostatic union once, in accessible language: one divine Person with a fully divine nature and a fully human nature. Avoid half-God/half-human explanations or two persons cooperating.

This retrieves the Incarnation rather than restarting it. The source’s Nativity artwork illustrates this truth; the lecture uses original inline symbols so it remains offline.

His life and teaching, death and Resurrection reveal God and accomplish salvation. The three offices help us understand this one saving mission.

4. Christ means Anointed One

Read Luke 4:16–21, including Jesus’ statement after he reads Isaiah. Ask students to identify the poor, captives, blind and oppressed.

Explain the background of anointing: consecration or being set apart for God’s work. Christ is a title, not Jesus’ surname.

Luke 4 foregrounds Jesus’ Spirit-filled proclamation and liberation. The whole biblical witness, not this passage alone, grounds all three offices.

Connect the chrism from Lesson 1.6 to sharing Christ’s mission. The Holy Spirit gives the grace; oil is the sacramental sign.

CCC 783–786: Christ and the mission of his people

5. Three offices, one saving mission

Preview Priest, Prophet, King in the maintained lecture order. The teacher note groups Priest, King, Prophet; both explain the same three offices.

An office means a role or mission, not an administrative room. The roles overlap: the Cross is priestly self-offering, royal victory and a revelation of divine love.

Invite students to keep a three-column note with Jesus’ action, a Scripture reference and a Christian response.

CCC 783–786

6. The priest who offers himself

Read Hebrews 9:11–14. Briefly explain sanctuary, redemption and conscience before asking for a comparison. Redemption means deliverance from bondage through Christ’s saving action.

Jesus is both High Priest and offering. His sacrifice is not merely an example of bravery; it is the saving act that reconciles us to God.

Connect to Lesson 1.5: Passion, Death and Resurrection are the center of the Paschal Mystery. The Cross is freely embraced in love.

Explain the Christian reading of Israel’s sacrificial worship respectfully, as preparation in salvation history. Do not describe Jewish people as rejected by God or imply modern Jewish worship centers on animal sacrifice.

7. One sacrifice, made present

Use the sequence to connect the note’s Last Supper illustration with the Cross and the Eucharist today. The events are related, but the Mass does not add a new death of Jesus.

CCC 1362–1367 explains sacramental memorial: the unique sacrifice of Christ is made present. The manner of offering is sacramental.

The Resurrection belongs to the Paschal Mystery; the living, risen Christ is present in the Eucharist.

Lay participation includes joining prayer, work and daily life to Christ’s offering. Only an ordained priest or bishop consecrates the Eucharist.

CCC 1362–1368: the Eucharistic sacrifice

8. Christ intercedes for us

Define mediator: the one who brings humanity into communion with God. Jesus does this uniquely as the incarnate Son through his saving self-offering.

Hebrews 7:25 and 9:24 develop the source’s claim that he continually intercedes. Intercession does not mean his sacrifice was incomplete.

The sacraments are actions of Christ in the Church. Reconciliation applies his forgiveness through the ordained minister; it does not give the minister an independent power to save.

Our prayers for others participate in Christ’s intercession. They do not compete with his unique mediation.

9. Offer your daily life

Read Romans 12:1–2. A living sacrifice means the whole person offered to God through a transformed life, not seeking physical harm.

Examples from the note: intercession, prayer, work, joys and sufferings, holiness and participation in the Eucharist. A student can offer honest effort, attention and care while studying.

Offering suffering never requires remaining in abuse or refusing help. Christians also seek relief for suffering and work against injustice.

Clarify common and ministerial priesthood. Ordination gives a distinct sacramental ministry serving the baptismal life of the whole Church.

Suggested answer: the Christian student offers the day to God, prays for a struggling classmate, and acts with integrity rather than treating worship as separate from school.

CCC 897–903: lay vocation and priestly participation

10. He reveals the Father

Read John 1:14–18. Connect directly to Lesson 1.4: the same Incarnation that makes Jesus truly human grounds his unique revelation of the Father.

The note also uses John 14:9. Explain that the Son reveals the Father; he is not the same divine Person as the Father. Preserve the Trinity distinction from Lesson 1.3.

Biblical prophets called people back to covenant faithfulness, often at great personal cost. Jesus fulfills this work and surpasses it as the eternal Word.

Optional short follow-up: John 14:8–10 if the Father/Son distinction needs clarification.

11. Teaching that calls for a response

The source describes parables, sermons, moral teaching, foretelling, and the challenge to injustice and hypocrisy. Keep the examples concrete rather than reducing prophet to future-teller.

Matthew 7:24–29 concludes the Sermon on the Mount with hearing and doing, followed by the crowd’s response to his authority.

Other source references for discussion: Matthew 21:11, the crowds identify Jesus as a prophet; Luke 4:24, rejection in his own country; Matthew 16:21, the prediction of suffering and rising.

Confronting injustice is compatible with loving persons. Christian truth-telling should not become humiliating people or claiming moral superiority.

12. Christ continues to guide his Church

Read John 16:12–15. Place it in Jesus’ farewell discourse before the Passion, as a promise of the Spirit’s continuing presence with his disciples.

The note explains that Christ’s prophetic mission continues through the Holy Spirit and the Church’s preaching after the Ascension. Link to the final movement of Lesson 1.5.

Catholic teaching receives Scripture within apostolic Tradition, with the Magisterium serving the word of God. Avoid presenting personal intuition as a new revelation that replaces Christ.

The faithful should listen, study and ask honest questions. The distinction in the deeper connection protects against treating every individual opinion as Christ’s own voice.

CCC 888–892; 904–907: teaching and prophetic witness

13. Speak truth with love

Use examples from the note: explaining faith, teaching, loving advice, encouraging someone toward goodness, advocacy for justice and a trustworthy daily example.

A student can correct a false rumor, acknowledge a mistake, explain a belief when asked, or stand against a degrading joke without demeaning the person who made it.

Evangelization includes explicitly sharing the Good News of Jesus; it is not simply any helpful act. A credible life and respectful words belong together.

The baptismal prophetic role does not mean every Christian has a personal gift of predicting the future. Nor does it permit coercion in matters of faith.

CCC 904–907: witness by word and life

14. A kingdom that will not end

The source calls Jesus King of the Universe, descendant of David, and King of kings. Luke 1 roots the claim in the promise to David; Revelation expresses his universal lordship.

Do not imply that Jesus first became divine at the Resurrection. The eternal Son’s victory and exaltation disclose his lordship in salvation history.

If students recall Palm Sunday, note that the donkey portrays a humble, peaceful king. The title King reaches its deepest Christian meaning through the Cross and Resurrection.

The Kingdom is already present in Christ and grows among his people, but its fullness is still awaited. It cannot be equated with any present political party or country.

15. A kingship of truth

Read John 18:33–37 in the NRSV-CE; use the wording of that translation rather than the note’s quotation from another version.

Set the scene: Pilate questions Jesus about kingship during the Passion. Jesus’ answer distinguishes the source and character of his authority from worldly domination.

The kingdom is spiritual and eternal, but not merely private or inward. Faith should shape social life, justice and care for neighbors.

Ask students to connect the truthful witness here with the prophetic role already studied. The offices overlap.

16. To reign is to serve

Read Matthew 20:25–28. Notice that the final verse connects service with Jesus giving his life as a ransom. Do not reduce the passage to generic leadership advice.

Optional extension: John 13:3–15, the foot washing. The source’s Ford Madox Brown image shows this scene; the lecture uses an inline basin and towel to keep the file self-contained.

Compare this servant kingship with the humble entry into Jerusalem and with the Cross from Lesson 1.5.

Service can include decisive action, speaking against injustice and protecting someone vulnerable. It is not passive acceptance of wrongdoing.

17. Lead yourself. Serve your neighbor.

Self-mastery means learning to govern desires and choices according to the good, not controlling other people. Link this to the virtues and continuing conversion from Lesson 1.6.

Examples from the note: a service project, befriending someone lonely, challenging bullying, promoting peace, and defending the dignity and rights of those in need.

Student leadership can be quiet and informal. A title or election is not required to include a classmate or complete an unglamorous task.

Distinguish charity and justice: immediate help matters, and so does addressing the practices that keep excluding people.

CCC 908–913: participation in Christ’s kingly office

18. Who gets left out?

Give pairs about two minutes, then invite more than one answer before revealing the model. The goal is justified application, not guessing exact wording.

Priestly participation is expressed in offering and prayer; prophecy in truthful witness; kingship in service and justice. One action may express more than one office.

Ask how to address both the immediate exclusion and the club practice that allowed it. Listen to the person affected rather than deciding everything for them.

Everyone has human dignity, regardless of Baptism or faith. Students may analyze the scenario hypothetically without disclosing their own religious identity.

Fuller model retained from the previous revealed response: Pray and offer the work for others (priestly); name the exclusion truthfully and charitably (prophetic); invite the student into planning and use leadership to support participation (royal). The roles are aspects of one mission, not competing identities.

19. Which calling needs practice?

This consolidates the source’s Think Deeper and final reflection prompts. Ask which role feels easiest, which is hardest, and how practicing all three could support balanced discipleship.

Connect to trust: Christ the Priest reveals his self-giving love; the King gives hope in his victory; the Prophet guides toward truth. These are reasons for confidence, not a promise of a trouble-free life.

A useful plan names an action, a context and a time. The revealed model illustrates an integrated response; it is not the only correct answer.

Offer a hypothetical alternative for students who do not wish to disclose personal beliefs or experiences. Assess understanding and the quality of reasoning, not religious commitment.

20. One Lord. A shared mission.

Model: Priest—Jesus offers himself once for all and reconciles us; King—he reigns in love and serves, victorious over sin and death; Prophet—he is the incarnate Word who reveals the Father and teaches truth.

Baptism incorporates Christians into Christ and gives a share in his mission. Participation is enabled by grace and does not make a person another savior, another divine Person or an ordained priest.

Accept a Scripture-supported explanation such as Hebrews 9 showing redemption through Christ’s self-offering, or John 1 showing the Son revealing God.

Assess accuracy of the three roles, the Scripture connection, the baptismal explanation and the reasoned practical example. The supplied note’s understanding questions are distributed across slide prompts and this exit response.

Bridge to 1.8: Baptism gives a particular Christian mission, while service extends to all human beings. The next lesson examines humanity and the dignity every person already possesses, regardless of faith, ability or status.
