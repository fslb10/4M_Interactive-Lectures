# Unit 1.2 — Causation Theory

20-slide lecture based on the supplied **Unit 1.2.docx**. Allow approximately 60–70 minutes, or divide after slide 12. Seven Scripture pauses use an NRSV-CE Bible. Slides provide references and listening prompts, with fuller interpretation in presenter notes.

## Learning goals

Students distinguish primary and secondary causality, explain genuine natural causes and human freedom, outline Aquinas's efficient-cause argument, and apply divine providence without excusing wrongdoing or encouraging passivity. The lesson connects God's sustaining action with a free response to grace and the saving work of Christ.

## Suggested classroom sequence

Begin with an event that has several causes. Use the candle on slide 3 to identify a choice, a physical process and an effect. Ask what evidence would establish a cause rather than only a sequence. An imagined example is sufficient; no flame demonstration is required.

After slides 4–6, ask students to explain the same event using primary and secondary causality. A successful answer names the real created cause and explains sustaining dependence without adding God as a competing physical force.

Slide 7 is the first paired activity. Before revealing, ask what the artist/brush analogy shows and where it fails. Extend orally to the electricity/appliance and musician/instrument analogies from the note. The essential limit is that persons can understand and choose, while God is not another material agent inside the system.

Slides 8–12 connect work, natural processes, freedom and grace. Identify what a person can influence without claiming complete control of results. Read both verses of Philippians 2:12–13 together. Do not interpret moral effort as purchasing salvation or grace as a reason for passivity.

On slides 13–14, ask students to state a premise and formulate an objection fairly. “Every infinite series is impossible” is not an adequate explanation of Aquinas's argument. Keep chronological succession distinct from an ordered dependence of causal powers.

Slides 16–18 require care. Allow questions and acknowledge that primary and secondary causality do not supply a complete explanation of each tragedy. The lesson's proper practical response includes resisting harm, providing care, lament and hope. Do not ask students to explain a personal bereavement or trauma.

Slide 19 is the main application. Before revealing, require a correction of fatalism, a concrete action and a statement of what remains outside the student's control. Students may extend this to the note's examples of care, healing, coincidences and answered prayer without claiming knowledge of a hidden divine intention.

Slide 20 assesses distinction, reasoning, providence and application. Model answers are in presenter notes. Assess accurate explanation and reasoning rather than personal religious assent.

## Teaching clarifications

- **Cause and evidence:** An effect means something caused. “Every effect has a cause” does not by itself prove that every event has a particular kind of cause. A prior event or correlation is not sufficient evidence of causation.
- **Primary and secondary:** Secondary does not mean unreal or trivial. God is understood as the source of being and action; creatures exercise real powers. These are not percentages of one physical force. Saying that God sustains a creature does not supply a new scientific mechanism.
- **Continual dependence:** God is neither simply the earliest domino nor a physical energy supply. The brush, electricity and musician analogies each illuminate a feature and each have limits. The exact modern analogies are not presented as verbatim Aquinas quotations.
- **Nature and law:** Physical laws and moral natural law are distinct. Description of what occurs does not alone determine what ought to occur. Order does not require complete predictability or exclude contingency and chance within created processes.
- **Freedom and results:** Studying contributes to learning but does not guarantee a grade. Moral choices shape character and relationships without guaranteeing comfort, prosperity or immediate visible reward. Failure and suffering do not prove guilt or weak faith.
- **Responsibility:** The note's “100 percent responsible” shorthand is replaced with the more careful account of knowledge, freedom and circumstances in CCC 1735. Coercion, ignorance and other factors can reduce personal responsibility. Dignity does not depend on achievement or independence.
- **Aquinas:** The Second Way concerns efficient causation and an ordered causal series. It is not simply a claim that a chronological sequence must have a first member. Aquinas did not think reason alone could demonstrate a beginning of the world in time; he distinguished that revealed belief from causal dependence. State the argument fairly and permit examination of its premises.
- **Other positions:** Occasionalism denies genuine created efficient causes. Deism's detached clockmaker picture is different from materialism. Catholic teaching is not merely a numerical midpoint between extremes; it affirms sustaining divine action together with real created agency. Miracles are not denied, but an unexplained event alone does not demonstrate one.
- **Providence and evil:** The note's phrase “against His permissive will” is corrected. Permission is not approval or positive willing of wrongdoing. God does not cause moral evil as moral evil. Sustaining a creature's power to act is distinguished from the defect of its wrongful choice.
- **Suffering:** Free-will reasoning addresses moral evil but does not by itself explain every natural disaster, disease or other suffering. Romans 8:28 is not a claim that each event is good or that its purpose is evident. Do not tell a sufferer that the harm was deserved or necessary for a lesson.
- **The Cross:** God's saving action brings good from evil without making injustice good. The Acts passage must not become collective blame of Jewish people. Jesus' self-gift and Resurrection ground Christian hope; they do not authorize doing evil for an intended good.
- **Grace and prayer:** Human response matters, and grace makes that response possible. Prayer can accompany effort, expertise and appropriate care. An outcome or coincidence does not prove a particular divine intention. Catholic schools differ in technical explanations of grace and freedom; this introductory lesson preserves their shared affirmations.
- **Source use:** Unresolved numbered footnotes and unsupported exact quotations in the note are not reproduced as verified citations. Scriptural prompts use NRSV-CE references; Catechism summaries are paraphrases unless explicitly identified otherwise.
