Essential teaching clarifications

Retain the exact original combined readings: Exodus with 1 Corinthians 5, Mark with John 15, and Romans with 1 Corinthians 11. Distinguish their settings. Christ’s free love gives his suffering saving meaning; pain does not earn salvation. Father and Son share one saving purpose, and Jewish people bear no collective guilt for the Crucifixion. Resurrection is glorified bodily life, distinct from resuscitation and from a metaphor for personal growth. Christ remains human after Ascension. Eucharist makes his one sacrifice present without another death. The teacher note’s Paschal-candle custom is historical: current Roman practice continues its Easter use through Pentecost. Bethany and the blessing come from Luke 24, not Acts 1. Hope does not guarantee immediate relief or require remaining in harm.

Teaching priorities and assessment

Teach the stated question, the complete spoken core where supplied, and the seven full recordings. Offer the case before its model response; assess accurate explanation and a reasoned action, not personal faith or disclosure. Short core assessments do not require optional council dates, additional readings or liturgical details. The full version permits discussion and deeper connections. Suggested full pacing: 5 minutes retrieval, 40 minutes teaching and readings, 10 minutes application, 5 minutes exit/bridge; allow up to 10 extra minutes for passage discussions. Short lessons include two 30-second responses; lengthen them when the class needs more time.

Slide 2 · 1 Corinthians 15:3–4

Context

  • Paul reminds the Corinthian Christian community of the gospel he received and passed on.
  • These two verses name Christ’s death for sins, burial and rising. The chapter continues with witnesses and an argument about resurrection.
  • The recording ends at verse 4. Witness lists and the later firstfruits argument belong to the surrounding chapter, not this short excerpt.

Meaning

  • The Cross and Resurrection belong together in the saving message. Burial underscores that Christ’s death was real.
  • The four-movement map includes Passion and Ascension as well; this excerpt anchors its central death-and-rising proclamation.
  • When explaining a belief, connect its claims rather than listing disconnected events. Ask what each event contributes.

Discuss: Why would separating the Cross from Easter change the Christian message?

Slide 3 · Exodus 12:5–13; 1 Corinthians 5:7

Context

  • Exodus presents God’s Passover instructions through Moses and Aaron to Israel before departure from slavery in Egypt.
  • The meal and blood on the doorposts belong to the liberation narrative. The recording then moves to a different text and community.
  • In 1 Corinthians, Paul uses Passover imagery while challenging wrongdoing in the Church. His reference to Christ is a Christian interpretation.

Meaning

  • Paschal draws on Passover. Christian typology reads the earlier liberation in relation to Christ’s saving work.
  • This slide distinguishes Israel’s Exodus from the Christian claim of liberation from sin and death; the settings are not interchangeable.
  • Respect Passover as a living Jewish observance with its own meaning. Christian interpretation does not erase Jewish faith or dignity.

Discuss: What connection does the comparison make, and what difference must it preserve?

Slide 4 · Luke 22:14–20

Context

  • Luke presents Jesus at a Passover meal with the apostles before his arrest and death.
  • The bread and cup are interpreted in relation to his self-gift and the new covenant. His words connect meal and coming Passion.
  • The excerpt ends with the cup of the covenant. Luke’s later Gethsemane account is a separate scene and is not part of this recording.

Meaning

  • Covenant means a binding relationship. The Last Supper presents Jesus’s coming death as a free offering for others.
  • This slide prepares the later Eucharist connection: Catholic worship participates in Christ’s one saving sacrifice.
  • Self-giving love seeks another’s good. It never makes the violence done to Jesus, or to another person, morally right.

Discuss: Why does the meal help explain the meaning of the Cross?

Slide 5 · Mark 14:32–36; John 15:13

Context

  • In Mark, Jesus prays in Gethsemane with Peter, James and John nearby after the supper and before his arrest.
  • His distress and prayer show the reality of the approaching Passion. The cup images what he faces; his obedience is freely offered.
  • The recording then adds John 15:13, from Jesus’s farewell teaching to disciples. It is not another sentence spoken in Mark’s garden scene.

Meaning

  • Passion means suffering; its saving meaning comes from Christ’s free love, not from pain as a good in itself.
  • Recall 1.4: Christ has a real human will. Genuine anguish and natural aversion to death do not mean sinful opposition to God.
  • Self-giving care can be costly, but love also protects people. Seeking help or leaving a harmful situation is compatible with discipleship.

Discuss: How can real anguish coexist with a freely chosen act of love?

Slide 7 · John 19:28–30

Context

  • John places Jesus on the Cross after his trial and Roman sentencing. His mother and the beloved disciple appear just before this excerpt.
  • The verses describe thirst, offered wine and the completion of his mission in real death.
  • This final saying is John’s account. Do not imply that all four Gospels give identical words or that this excerpt includes the burial.

Meaning

  • The divine Son truly dies in his humanity. Christian teaching understands his death as a free, loving offering for reconciliation.
  • The slide links completion with the one sacrifice; the Resurrection will reveal the new life this saving work opens.
  • God’s saving purpose never excuses the execution or assigns collective guilt to Jewish people. Respond to suffering with care, not blame.

Discuss: What does the Christian claim of a completed mission add to the account of an execution?

1. The Paschal Mystery

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

Guiding question: How can a death bring life? Recall the Roman purpose of crucifixion, then let students consider why the Cross became a Christian sign of hope. Return to their answers at the end.

Paschal comes from Pascha, meaning Passover. The events are one unified mystery of salvation, not disconnected miracles.

Seven complete recorded readings and synchronized captions are embedded. Students may follow in their NRSV-CE Bibles; other references are optional extensions.

Plan 60–70 minutes, including approximately six minutes of Scripture audio. Retrieve the Incarnation: the same divine Son truly suffers, dies and rises in his humanity. This is why identity in 1.4 leads to saving work in 1.5. Pause after slide 11 if teaching across two periods.

2. The whole story of salvation

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

Use this slide as the route through the events. The word definitions have already been established. Ask students how each movement contributes to the one saving work.

The Paschal Mystery comprises Passion, Death, Resurrection and Ascension; the latter includes Christ’s entrance into heavenly glory.

In the Catholic understanding, the Cross and Resurrection are inseparable at the heart of salvation.

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

7. The Cross: “It is finished.”

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

Avoid implying every Gospel reports exactly the same final saying; “It is finished” is specifically the Johannine account.

Read John 19:28–30 aloud. Pause and ask what is finished: not Jesus’ influence but his saving self-gift.

Crucifixion was shameful public punishment in the Roman world. The Christian claim is a radical reversal of its meaning.

Do not attribute collective responsibility for Jesus’ death to Jewish people, then or now. The execution was Roman; individual historical responsibility cannot be inferred for a whole people. God’s saving purpose does not make betrayal, torture or killing morally good (CCC 597–600).

8. Christ’s one sacrifice

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

Hebrews presents Jesus as both High Priest and the one who offers himself. Explain sacrifice as his complete gift of love and obedience to the Father for us.

Read Romans 5:18–19 to develop the New Adam connection. Paul sets Adam’s disobedience beside Christ’s obedience and its consequences for humanity.

The Father and Son share one saving purpose. The Cross does not portray a loving Son persuading an unwilling Father to love us.

Once for all means that Christ’s sacrifice does not need repeating. The Eucharist makes it sacramentally present, a connection developed on the sacrament slide.

Qualify the source note’s punishment/payment language: the Father and Son share one loving purpose; the innocent Son does not become morally guilty or hated by the Father. Catholic teaching locates saving value in the divine Person’s free love and obedience, not in a quantity of pain (CCC 603–616).

9. Salvation: to be rescued

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

Begin with ordinary meanings of rescue and deliverance, then recall how the Exodus is a concrete story of salvation in Israel’s history.

The New Testament expands the meaning to forgiveness of sin, reconciliation with God, liberation from death and a share in divine life.

Connect salvation with grace. In Catholic teaching it is initiated by God, received in faith, and lived through an ongoing response of love and cooperation with grace.

Sin damages communion with God and neighbour. Human effort alone cannot heal that rupture or conquer death. God takes the initiative, and grace enables our free response. CCC 1996–2002.

10. The curtain tears

11. Jesus really entered death

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

This is a short interlude, not an additional event replacing the four-part framework.

Do not explain the Resurrection as mere symbolic survival of Jesus’ ideas; the Church teaches a real death followed by bodily Resurrection.

Christ’s death is the real separation of his human soul and body; the divine Person remains united to both. Descent to the dead affirms his saving work reaches the righteous who preceded him; it is not a second crucifixion (CCC 624–635).

15. The Ascension: our humanity with God

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

The “forty days” comes from Acts 1. The Catechism describes the definitive entrance of Jesus’ humanity into heavenly glory (CCC 659, 665).

Explain that Ascension does not mean Jesus becomes divine for the first time. The Incarnation united divinity and humanity from the beginning.

Christ’s ongoing intercession means the saving work remains effective and the Church is sent on mission.

Recall John 1:14 and John 14:2–3 from the Incarnation connection. The humanity Jesus assumed is the humanity that enters glory. Jesus remains fully God and fully human.

Participation in divine life is a gift of grace. It does not erase human identity or make people independent gods.

Acts 1:3 gives the forty days. Acts 1:6–11 links Ascension with the promise of the Spirit, the Church’s mission and Christ’s return.

Source-note extension: Acts 1:6–11 gives the commission, cloud and promise of return; the Bethany location and blessing appear in Luke 24:50–53. Avoid presenting a harmonized account as the exact words of Acts. Hebrews 9:24–28, John 14:2–3, John 16:7, Matthew 28:19–20, Ephesians 1:20–22, John 18:36, Luke 1:32–33 and Colossians 3:1–2 extend intercession, kingship and mission; they are not additional recordings.

Optional distinction: Christ’s Ascension and Mary’s Assumption are not identical. Mary is taken into heavenly glory by God’s gift; she shares her Son’s victory (CCC 966). This extension is not required for the short assessment.

Liturgical correction to the teacher note: in the current Roman rite the Paschal candle is lit for solemn celebrations through Pentecost, not generally extinguished for the season at Ascension. Treat the note’s Ascension custom as historical, not a current universal rule.

16. Sharing in Christ’s saving work

Catholics believe that the Eucharist truly is Christ’s Body and Blood. Participation draws the Church into his offering and nourishes communion with him. CCC 1324, 1362–1367.

18. Living the Paschal pattern

Reading priority: only slides labelled CORE require a new reading. Other passages are recall or optional extension; use the listening question before supplying an interpretation.

Bring the doctrine into Grade 12 experience without implying all hardship is holy or deserved.

Examples: costly forgiveness, protecting a vulnerable student, self-giving care in family life, service to people in need.

Keep grace before works: Christian action flows from participation in Christ, not a claim to earn heaven on one’s own.

Allow a historical or hypothetical example rather than requiring disclosure of personal suffering. Suffering does not automatically produce growth, and Christian love includes resisting harm and seeking help.

Connect Christian action with grace: faith, hope and charity express an ongoing response to God. Forgiveness, service and justice put that response into practice.

Retain source-note extensions Luke 9:23 (discipleship), Ephesians 2:13 (reconciliation), Matthew 28:20 (Christ’s presence), and Philippians 3:21 (bodily transformation). Reconciliation/Confession, repentance, Lent, Holy Week, Easter and Sunday worship express participation; the liturgical year is optional enrichment. Hope is not a promise that every hardship soon turns into success.
