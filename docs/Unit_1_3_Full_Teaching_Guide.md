## Essential teaching clarifications

One nature, three distinct Persons, each fully divine: reject three gods, parts and changing roles. Transcendence is not distance, immanence is not pantheism, and holiness does not exclude mercy toward sinners. Distinguish presence, knowledge and power. The threefold holy is not by itself a proof of the Trinity. Contradictions are not possible tasks; unanswered prayer is not a measure of someone’s faith. Fatherhood is not a male divine body. Love includes justice, boundaries and a free response.

## Context and Meaning

### Slide 3 · Deuteronomy 6:4–5

**Context**

- Deuteronomy presents Moses addressing Israel before entry into the promised land, renewing covenant instruction.
- The Shema calls Israel to hear and love the one God wholeheartedly. It remains central in Jewish faith and prayer.
- The following verses call for teaching these words in daily life. Our recording contains the opening call, verses 4–5.

**Meaning**

- Monotheism means belief in one God. The call involves the whole person, not simply knowing a definition.
- This slide establishes the unity that Christian Trinitarian teaching must preserve. Christianity receives Israel’s Scriptures without erasing Judaism.
- A belief shapes priorities when it changes how someone treats others, spends time or responds to a difficult choice.

Discuss: What would distinguish knowing about love of God from living that commitment?

### Slide 5 · Matthew 28:16–20

**Context**

- At the end of Matthew, the eleven disciples meet the risen Jesus on a mountain in Galilee.
- The scene includes worship and doubt. Jesus commissions them to make disciples, baptize and teach.
- Father, Son and Holy Spirit are named in the baptismal commission. Jesus closes with a promise of continuing presence.

**Meaning**

- Catholic faith confesses one divine nature and three distinct Persons, each fully God.
- This slide connects that belief with baptismal language. The Church’s formulation draws on revelation as a whole, not a mathematical puzzle.
- Communion with God calls for learning and living Jesus’s teaching. A person can ask honest questions while continuing to learn.

Discuss: Why would “three parts of God” misrepresent the baptismal names?

### Slide 8 · Isaiah 6:1–8

**Context**

- Isaiah recounts a vision in the year King Uzziah died: the Lord enthroned and seraphim proclaiming holiness.
- The prophet acknowledges unclean lips. A seraph touches his lips with a coal as a sign of cleansing and forgiveness.
- He then hears a call and offers to be sent. The vision joins worship, mercy and vocation.

**Meaning**

- Holiness includes God’s incomparable greatness and perfect goodness. The vision does not give a literal diagram of God’s body.
- This slide connects awe with forgiveness and service. God’s holiness does not make approach to sinners impossible.
- A practical response is to admit a wrong, accept correction and do good. Reverence should affect conduct, not only outward appearance.

Discuss: How does Isaiah move from awareness of a failing to willingness to serve?

### Slide 10 · Psalm 139:1–12

**Context**

- The psalmist addresses God in prayer, reflecting on being known in ordinary movements, thoughts and speech.
- Images of heaven, Sheol and the farthest sea express the reach of God’s presence. Sheol is the realm of the dead.
- The excerpt ends with darkness unable to hide from God. Later verses continue the meditation on being formed and known.

**Meaning**

- Omniscience concerns knowing all truth; omnipresence concerns presence to all creation. The two ideas are related but distinct.
- This slide reads both attributes through care and trust. God is not a fluid spread through space or identical with creation.
- Honest prayer can acknowledge what is difficult. Someone who feels alone can also seek companionship and practical support.

Discuss: Which line suggests knowledge, and which suggests presence?

[Psalm 139:1–12 — chapter and commentary](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/psalms/139)

### Slide 12 · Luke 1:26–38

**Context**

- Luke places Gabriel’s visit to Mary in Nazareth after the announcement of John the Baptist’s birth.
- The angel announces Jesus’s birth and royal mission. Mary asks how this will happen and hears of the Holy Spirit and Elizabeth.
- The scene ends with Mary’s willing response. The reassurance about divine power belongs to this saving promise.

**Meaning**

- Omnipotence means divine power without created limits, always consistent with truth and goodness.
- The slide connects power with salvation and Mary’s response. It does not promise that every desired outcome must happen.
- Trust can include a sincere question and a responsible response. Faith is not a technique for controlling events.

Discuss: How do Mary’s question and response help distinguish trust from passivity?

### Slide 15 · 1 John 4:7–12

**Context**

- The letter addresses Christian readers about faith, discernment and love within the community.
- It urges love of one another and points to God’s initiative in sending the Son as the revelation of love.
- The excerpt moves from God’s gift to the readers’ responsibility to love; it ends with God dwelling in those who love.

**Meaning**

- God’s love comes before our deserving it. Love seeks another’s good and is more than a passing feeling.
- This slide joins divine identity with action: knowledge, power and holiness belong to the same loving God.
- Protect someone’s dignity through patient attention or practical care. Being loved does not depend on achievement or popularity.

**Context**

- John places these verses within the conversation between Jesus and Nicodemus, a Jewish teacher who visits at night.

## Presenter notes and teaching script

Core scripts appear first in short lessons. Additional notes give depth for discussion; optional material is outside the short core estimate. Students can explain a perspective without disclosing personal beliefs or experiences.


### 1. Beyond all things. Near to each person.

Goals: explain monotheism and the Trinity; distinguish transcendence and immanence; describe holiness, presence, knowledge, power and personal relationship; connect these attributes with saving love.

Allow approximately 60–70 minutes. Seven Scripture pauses occur on slides 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 15 and 16. A two-part lesson can pause after slide 10.

Use the students’ NRSV-CE Bible. The slides provide references and listening questions, not printed passages. Notes supply nuance without crowding the screen.

Evaluate accurate explanation and thoughtful reasoning. Students may reflect hypothetically; no disclosure of personal faith or private struggles is required.

Batch 1: all seven complete embedded Scripture readings and synchronized captions are available. Context and Meaning are optional discussion panels; opening them pauses audio without losing the position.


### 2. From existence and action to identity

Ask students to explain one distinction from each earlier lesson: necessary and contingent being; primary and secondary causality. Accept a brief example rather than replaying the arguments.

A First Cause argument alone does not establish the Trinity. Distinguish what philosophical reasoning proposes from what Christians confess through revelation.

In 1.2, being dependent on God did not make created action unreal. In 1.3, God’s greatness does not make relationship impossible.

This lesson prepares for the Incarnation in 1.4 and the Paschal Mystery in 1.5. Later lessons on Baptism and Christian life develop participation in this saving relationship.


### 3. One God, wholehearted love

Read Deuteronomy 6:4–5, the opening of the Shema. Explain its central place in Jewish faith; Christianity receives these Scriptures and Jesus affirms this command. Do not present Judaism as a discarded stage.

The note also names Isaiah 45:5 and Exodus 3:13–15. The divine name YHWH is associated with God’s self-disclosure to Moses; many English Bibles render it LORD. Respect Jewish practice concerning the name.

Exodus joins mystery and personal faithfulness: the God who cannot be reduced to a created object makes himself known and acts to free his people. This develops the distinction between dependent creatures and their source.

Optional follow-up: Exodus 3:13–15. Keep it optional so the seven main reading pauses remain manageable.

[CCC 200–213: one God and the divine name](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_1_i_believe_in_god.html)


### 4. Beyond creation, present within it

Transcendence is not a location very far away in space. God is not the largest object among other objects. Immanence does not make God a material ingredient of creation.

Connect directly with 1.1: necessary being does not receive existence as creatures do. Connect with 1.2: sustaining creation is an ongoing dependence, not only a first event.

Avoid deism understood as a distant creator who leaves creation alone, and pantheism, which identifies God with the universe. The Christian claim holds distinction and presence together.

All three divine Persons share the one divine nature. Do not assign transcendence only to the Father and nearness only to the Son or Spirit.

[CCC 300: beyond creation and present to it](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_4_the_creator.index.html)


### 5. The one name, three divine Persons

Read Matthew 28:16–20. Include the setting with the risen Jesus, the disciples’ worship and doubt, the commission and the promise of presence.

The passage is a foundation for Trinitarian faith and baptismal practice. The later technical vocabulary of person and nature expresses the Church’s reflection on the whole witness of revelation.

Explain Trinity as one God in three distinct Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Do not infer three gods from three personal names.

This anticipates Baptism in 1.6. Here the focus is God’s identity, not a full repetition of sacramental effects or rites.

[CCC 232–237: the mystery of the Trinity](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_2_the_father.index.html)


### 6. One divine nature, real distinction

At this level, nature answers what God is; Person distinguishes who the Father, Son and Spirit are in relation to one another. These terms do not mean three independent human individuals sharing a species.

The Son is eternally begotten, not created. The Holy Spirit is a divine Person, not an impersonal energy. The Persons are not successive appearances of one Person.

A mystery is not a contradiction that faith excuses. Christian doctrine does not say one Person and three Persons in the same respect; it distinguishes one nature and three Persons.

The Nicene Creed provides the Church’s shared language. Reason can clarify meaning and reject misunderstandings; the Trinity is not deduced simply from observing nature.

[CCC 253–260: divine unity, distinction and action](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_2_the_father.index.html)


### 7. What does the triangle get wrong?

Give pairs one minute before revealing. A triangle can remind us of three and one, but its sides are parts. This is a correction of the source analogy, not a new proof of the Trinity.

Water changing between ice, liquid and vapor can suggest one Person switching modes. Three separate people can suggest three gods. All familiar analogies need explicit limits.

Do not replace one misleading picture with a new supposedly perfect one. Students should be able to explain the three doctrinal claims in ordinary language.

Connect with 1.2’s artist and brush activity: an analogy can illuminate one feature while distorting another. Good reasoning asks where the comparison stops.


### 8. Awe, purification and a call

Read Isaiah 6:1–8. Explain that the throne, seraphim and coal belong to a prophetic vision. Do not treat them as a literal diagram of God’s body or location.

Holiness includes God’s incomparable greatness and moral perfection. Avoid the unverified etymological claim that holy simply comes from a word meaning to cut.

God’s holiness does not mean God is physically unable to be near sinners. The scene itself joins holiness with mercy, forgiveness and a call to service.

The threefold holy is received in Christian worship. Do not claim that repetition alone proves the Trinity or that Isaiah explicitly formulated later Trinitarian doctrine.


### 9. Reverence that changes our response

This applies transcendence rather than redefining it. The high God of Psalm 113 raises the poor; divine greatness should not be confused with indifference to vulnerable people.

Leviticus 19’s call to holiness occurs among commands about worship, justice and neighbourly responsibility. Holiness is not simply looking impressive or keeping distant from people considered unworthy.

Humility acknowledges limits of understanding without abandoning thought. This continues 1.1’s distinction between a reasoned claim and a total explanation of reality.

Ask for concrete examples such as honest work, protecting someone’s dignity or serving without attention. Avoid treating public religiosity as proof of moral goodness.


### 10. Nowhere beyond presence. Nothing beyond knowledge.

Read Psalm 139:1–12. Ask students to distinguish the two attributes rather than treating them as synonyms. Presence concerns where; knowledge concerns what is known.

Sheol means the realm of the dead in the psalm’s imagery. Do not reduce it to a metaphor for depression. The language of extremes expresses the reach of God’s presence.

Omnipresence does not mean God is a fluid spread through space or identical with the universe. God is present to creatures while remaining distinct from them.

General divine presence must be distinguished from God’s indwelling through grace and the unique sacramental presence of Christ in the Eucharist. Saying God is everywhere does not make all these modes identical.

The note includes Psalm 147:5 and 1 John 3:20 as further knowledge references. Treat perfect knowledge as joined to wisdom and love, not as the surveillance of a suspicious human observer.

[CCC 300: God’s presence to creation](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_4_the_creator.index.html)


### 11. Known by God, still called to respond

Jesus teaches that the Father knows our needs before we ask. That is a reason for trusting prayer, not a command to stop praying.

God’s knowledge is not acquired through learning or waiting for new information as ours is. Catholic thought understands God as knowing without the temporal limits creatures have.

The statement knowing is not forcing identifies an important distinction; it does not settle every philosophical question about foreknowledge and freedom. Acknowledge the remaining difficulty honestly.

Connect with 1.2: dependence on God does not remove created agency. Avoid a fixed-script picture that turns every human wrongdoing into something God positively commanded.


### 12. Power joined to a saving promise

Read Luke 1:26–38. Keep the reassurance in its narrative context: the Annunciation and the promise of the birth of Jesus.

The note also names Genesis 18:14 and Jeremiah 32:17. These express confidence in God’s power; they are not a guarantee that every desired outcome will follow from sufficient confidence.

Mary asks a question and freely responds. Connect with 1.2’s attention to grace and human response without portraying her as a passive instrument.

The Creed’s profession of the Father Almighty does not exclude the Son and Spirit from divine omnipotence. Power belongs to the one divine nature.

[CCC 268–278: divine omnipotence](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_3_the_almighty.html)


### 13. Can God make a square circle?

Allow discussion before revealing. Aquinas distinguishes things that are possible in themselves from combinations of words that cancel the conditions of their own meaning.

Keep the qualifier in the same sense. A rounded square or an optical illusion is not a genuine example of the contradiction under discussion.

God’s inability to lie or do evil expresses perfect truth and goodness; it is not a missing capacity that would make God better. Power is not arbitrary domination.

Connect to the problem of evil from 1.2: affirming power and goodness does not explain why every specific suffering is permitted. Do not promise healing, prosperity or an easy life as proof of faith.

[CCC 271: power and goodness](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_3_the_almighty.html)

[Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I.25.3](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/1025.htm)


### 14. A relationship, not only an explanation

The note’s Good Shepherd and friendship themes may be developed through John 10:11–15 or John 15:15 if time permits. These are optional extensions, not extra required reading pauses.

Personal does not mean God is a fourth Person alongside Father, Son and Spirit. It distinguishes the living, relational God from an impersonal force.

Language about God’s hand, anger or compassion is analogical. Do not infer a divine body or unstable human moods. God’s concern is real without being identical to our bodily and emotional limitations.

Fatherhood is revealed language, not a statement that the divine nature is biologically male. The Incarnation is the Son’s real assumption of humanity, not a claim that the Father also became incarnate.

Prayer includes Scripture, worship and listening. Do not equate every inner feeling with God’s voice; discernment and formation matter.

[CCC 239–240: divine fatherhood](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_2_the_father.index.html)


### 15. Love belongs to who God is

Read 1 John 4:7–12. Distinguish the theological statement that God is love from the claim that every feeling called love is automatically good.

In the note, agape names self-giving love that seeks another’s good. Avoid suggesting that Greek vocabulary alone proves a complete doctrine or that love has only one emotional form.

God does not need creation to cure loneliness. Catholic teaching understands God as eternally full in the life of Father, Son and Spirit; creation is a free gift.

Holiness, knowledge, power, justice and love are not detachable parts competing within God. The attributes describe the one God from different aspects of our limited understanding.

Connect with dignity: being loved by God is not a prize for high achievement or independence. This prepares for the later lessons on humanity and the soul.

[CCC 218–221: God is love](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_1_i_believe_in_god.html)


### 16. The gift of the Son for the life of the world

Read John 3:16–17 together in the setting of Jesus’ conversation with Nicodemus. Verse 17 helps keep the focus on the saving purpose of the mission.

The Father sends the Son; the Son freely gives himself. They do not have opposing divine wills. Do not contrast an angry Father with a loving Son or imply that the Father became incarnate.

Romans 5:8 is an optional supporting reference: Christ’s saving love reaches sinners before they have made themselves deserving. Salvation begins in grace, not a human performance that purchases love.

Faith is more than knowing facts or saying a formula. In Catholic life, response to grace involves trust, conversion, sacramental life and love of neighbour. These will unfold in later lessons.

Preview 1.4’s Incarnation and 1.5’s Paschal Mystery. Here explain why God saves; those lessons develop how the Son’s human life, death and Resurrection accomplish salvation.


### 17. A love that welcomes and transforms

The note joins universal love with the seriousness of freedom. Do not interpret love as approval of abuse, dishonesty or injustice. Concern for the good includes resisting harm and protecting people.

Mercy does not erase responsibility. Forgiveness need not mean immediate reconciliation or abandoning appropriate boundaries and justice.

Catholic teaching describes hell as definitive self-exclusion from communion with God, involving free rejection of grace. Do not use this as a diagnosis of particular individuals or reduce complex questions of culpability to a moment of doubt.

Connect with 1.2: divine permission is not approval of evil. God’s power and love give hope without offering us a complete explanation for every instance of suffering.


### 18. What would trust sound like?

Allow pairs time to respond before revealing. A strong answer distinguishes being heard and loved from receiving every particular request.

The source asks how the attributes affect prayer. Omniscience can encourage honesty; presence supports prayer anywhere; personal love invites relationship; power supports hope beyond our own resources.

Connect directly with Lesson 1.2: prayer and practical action can belong together. Asking for help, preparing well and supporting others remain meaningful secondary causes.

Do not infer that suffering or disappointment indicates insufficient faith. Lament and unanswered questions can be part of prayer. Use hypothetical examples rather than asking students to recount painful experiences.


### 19. Attributes in use

Invite a short reflection, a question, or an optional prayer. Students may explain a Christian response in the third person instead of expressing personal belief.

Use the note’s conclusion: select an attribute that is meaningful or difficult and explain its effect on prayer and action. Require a definition and an example so the response goes beyond a feeling.

Charity is not merely an occasional donation; it is love of God and neighbour, shaping the whole moral life. Colossians 3:14 provides an optional concluding reference.

Return to the learning sequence: 1.1 considers existence, 1.2 sustaining action, 1.3 divine identity. The Incarnation and Paschal Mystery then show the saving love of this God in history.


### 20. Who is God, and why does it matter?

Model 1: there is one divine nature and three distinct Persons, each fully divine. Avoid parts, three gods or one Person acting three roles.

Model 2: transcendence means beyond created limits; omnipresence means present everywhere; omniscience means knowing all things; omnipotence means unlimited divine power consistent with truth and goodness. Include immanence as nearness and activity.

Model 3: God’s love initiates the gift of the Son for salvation and calls forth faith and love of neighbour. God’s nature and saving action belong together.

Model 4: because God already knows, prayer can be honest relationship; because created choices remain real, prayer can accompany preparation and service. Alternatively connect transcendence with the necessary/contingent distinction in 1.1.

Assess conceptual clarity, Scripture connection and thoughtful application. Note unresolved questions for the Incarnation lesson; do not treat doubt or a lack of personal assent as failure to understand.
