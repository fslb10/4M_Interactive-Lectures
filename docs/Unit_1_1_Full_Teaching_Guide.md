## Essential teaching clarifications

Keep observations distinct from philosophical inferences: the Big Bang model is not proof of creation from nothing; fine-tuning probabilities require assumptions; evolution and a theological account of creation are not competing mechanisms. Non-believers can act morally. Longing or a gap in scientific knowledge is not a proof by itself. Assess explanation and reasoning, not personal assent. The accepted pilot has a stale extension note saying 1.2–1.4 are absent; the current library contains them. Use the current rollout tracker for readiness.

## Context and Meaning

Every recorded reference has both visible controls. Context explains speaker/audience, setting and surrounding events. Meaning connects the passage to this slide and offers a practical question. Opening a panel pauses audio and preserves position; closing does not restart it. Press Play to resume. Sources below provide chapter context and Church teaching; the existing audio and subtitles remain NRSV-CE. USCCB chapter text is a different translation and may number Psalm headings differently.


### Slide 3 · Genesis 1:1–5

**Context**

- This is the opening of the Bible's first creation account. God speaks, light appears, and creation begins to take an ordered shape.
- The passage introduces a repeated pattern: God creates and recognizes creation as good.
- It is an ancient account about God and the world, rather than a modern description of physical processes.

**Meaning**

- The passage presents the world as dependent on God and fundamentally good.
- This slide begins with God’s initiative, order and goodness. It establishes the theological starting point before we examine cosmology and philosophical arguments.
- You can investigate nature seriously while also asking about meaning and purpose. Caring for the created world is one way to respond to its goodness.

Discuss: How is 'How did this develop?' different from 'Why does anything exist?'

[Genesis 1 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/genesis/1)

### Slide 6 · Wisdom 13:1–5

**Context**

- This Jewish wisdom text reflects on people who are amazed by nature's beauty and power.
- It questions treating things such as fire, stars, or the sea as gods in themselves.
- The writer invites readers to look through the beauty of created things toward their source, the Creator.

**Meaning**

- Wonder can lead to a deeper question: What is the source of all that exists?
- This connects with Aquinas on the slide: things that depend on others for their existence prompt a question about the ultimate source of that existence.
- The passage offers a religious way of understanding nature. It is an invitation to reason and reflect, not a substitute for scientific investigation or a complete proof by itself.

Discuss: What is the difference between explaining one thing in the universe and explaining why anything exists?

[Wisdom 13 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/wisdom/13)

### Slide 9 · Psalm 19:1–4

**Context**

- This psalm is a song of praise. Its opening imagines the sky and the cycle of day and night announcing God's glory.
- The heavens do not literally speak. The poet describes a witness that reaches people without ordinary words.
- Later, the psalm moves from the wonder of creation to God's instruction and a prayer about the speaker's own life.

**Meaning**

- Beauty and order can stir wonder and open questions about God.
- The slide connects this with arguments from order or design. The psalm expresses faith through poetry; it does not perform a scientific test or settle every objection.
- Paying attention to the world can encourage gratitude and care. A moment of wonder can become the start of a thoughtful question.

Discuss: When has something in nature made you stop and ask a bigger question?

[Psalm 19 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/psalms/19)

The USCCB page counts the psalm heading as verse 1. Its verses 2–5 correspond to the recorded NRSV-CE verses 1–4.

### Slide 11 · Romans 2:14–16

**Context**

- Paul is writing to Christians in Rome, including people from Jewish and non-Jewish backgrounds.
- He describes people who have not received the Jewish law but still recognize moral responsibilities.
- Their conscience bears witness to their choices. Paul's wider point is that God's judgment is fair.

**Meaning**

- People can recognize right and wrong even when they do not share the same religious beliefs.
- The slide asks whether our sense of moral responsibility points toward God. That is a question to examine, not proof that only religious people can be good.
- When something seems unfair, ask why. Giving reasons for your judgment helps you understand your conscience and listen respectfully to others.

Discuss: Why should we act fairly even when nobody will notice or reward us?

[Romans 2 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/romans/2)

### Slide 14 · Ecclesiastes 3:9–13

**Context**

- The speaker, often called the Teacher, asks what people gain from all their work. This follows the poem about a time for everything.
- People can think beyond the present, yet cannot grasp everything God is doing from beginning to end.
- Alongside these limits, the passage values doing good and enjoying ordinary gifts such as food and meaningful work.

**Meaning**

- Being human includes asking large questions while living with incomplete answers.
- The slide explores longing for truth, beauty and lasting happiness. Augustine interprets that longing as directed toward God; desire alone does not prove its object exists.
- You do not need every answer before doing something worthwhile today. Notice a gift, help someone, or take satisfaction in honest work.

Discuss: What gives an ordinary day meaning, even when bigger questions remain open?

[Ecclesiastes 3 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/ecclesiastes/3)

The recorded NRSV-CE verse 11 speaks of a sense of past and future. Avoid presenting the alternative wording 'eternity in their hearts' as a quotation from this recording.

### Slide 15 · Acts 9:1–9

**Context**

- Saul is travelling to Damascus to arrest followers of Jesus. He believes he is defending his faith.
- A light interrupts him, and the risen Jesus identifies himself with the people Saul is persecuting.
- Saul loses his sight and must be led into the city. The reading ends while he is waiting; Ananias's visit and Saul's baptism come later.

**Meaning**

- An encounter can challenge someone who feels completely certain and change the direction of a life.
- Saul's story connects with religious experience on this slide: an encounter changes his life. Powerful feelings still need thoughtful interpretation.
- Be open to reconsidering your assumptions. Reflect on an experience, seek wise guidance, and consider whether it leads toward truth, love, and responsibility.

Discuss: What might help you recognize that a deeply held assumption needs to change?

[Acts 9 — chapter context](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/acts/9)

### Slide 18 · Hebrews 1:1–3

**Context**

- Hebrews opens by recalling how God spoke through the prophets and then describing God's revelation through the Son.
- The writer presents the Son as revealing God's glory, sustaining creation, and bringing purification from sins.
- This is a Christian statement of faith about who Jesus is and why he matters.

**Meaning**

- The lesson now moves from 'Are there reasons to believe in God?' to 'How do Christians believe God becomes known?'
- The full lesson distinguishes natural knowledge of God from revelation in Christ. A philosophical argument about a source does not establish every Christian mystery.
- Reasoned questions and learning from revelation can work together. To understand Christian belief, examine Jesus's life and teaching as well as arguments about God's existence.

Discuss: How would learning about Jesus add to an argument that God exists?

## Presenter notes and teaching script

Core scripts appear first in short lessons. Additional notes give depth for discussion; optional material is outside the short core estimate. Students can explain a perspective without disclosing personal beliefs or experiences.


### 1. The existence of God

Learning goals: explain four approaches to God’s existence; distinguish scientific findings from philosophical conclusions; describe Catholic teaching on reason and revelation; evaluate an argument respectfully.

Allow about 65–75 minutes, or divide after slide 10. Seven reading pauses occur on slides 3, 6, 9, 11, 14, 15 and 18. Give time for student objections and questions.

This is the first lesson in sequence. Later-lesson connections are previews, or retrieval if teaching this after 1.5–1.12. No previous lecture is assumed.

Assess accurate explanation and reasoning, not a declaration of personal belief. Students may discuss a hypothetical perspective without disclosing private religious experience.

Batch 1: all seven complete embedded Scripture readings and synchronized captions are available. Context and Meaning are optional discussion panels; opening them pauses audio without losing the position.


### 2. Different methods, one search for truth

The note’s how/why distinction is a starting analogy, not an absolute boundary. Science also answers causal why questions. Philosophy and theology ask about ultimate explanation, value and purpose.

A scientist can study why rain falls; philosophy can ask why a law-governed world exists; faith can receive creation as gift. These are related questions with different methods.

Faith includes claims about reality and history, not only private feelings. Respectful dialogue allows evidence and reasoning to challenge mistaken interpretations.

[CCC 31–35](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_one/chapter_one/ii_ways_of_coming_to_know_god.index.html)

[CCC 36–38](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_one/chapter_one/iii_the_knowledge_of_god_according_to_the_church.index.html)


### 3. A world received as a gift

Read Genesis 1:1–5. Ask students to identify what the passage actually says before connecting it to a scientific model. The Bible uses literary forms suited to its theological purposes.

Do not use this text as a dated laboratory account of cosmic expansion. Its affirmation of God as Creator is distinct from a physical model of early cosmic history.

Creation is the setting of the whole salvation story: dignity, freedom, sin, covenant and redemption. Later lessons will unfold this relationship rather than treating God as only a remote first event.

[CCC 290–301](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_4_the_creator.index.html)


### 4. From what we encounter to what it may mean

Name the cosmological, teleological, moral and experiential approaches. They are families of arguments, not four laboratory tests.

Distinguish an observation, a premise, an inference and a conclusion. The same observation can receive different philosophical interpretations; discuss the reasons for choosing one.

Catholic teaching presents converging ways of knowing God. This does not mean every argument in the note is equally strong or that no thoughtful person can question a premise.


### 5. An expanding universe, an open question

Explain that expansion concerns space itself, not an explosion from a central location into an empty room. The cosmic microwave background is early light we observe today.

The model does not by itself establish creation from nothing or settle what, if anything, preceded the earliest physical conditions. A singularity is not an experimentally confirmed account of an absolute beginning.

Georges Lemaître, a Catholic priest and physicist, helped develop expanding-universe cosmology and the primeval-atom hypothesis. His example supports dialogue without turning cosmology into a direct proof of a religious doctrine.

Students can find harmony between a changing universe and belief in creation while recognizing that the philosophical argument requires additional premises.

[NASA: Webb and the Big Bang](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/big-bang-q-and-a/)

[Vatican Observatory: Lemaître and creation](https://www.vaticanobservatory.org/general/why-does-this-keep-happening-when-people-presume-the-big-bang-is-against-faith/)


### 6. Why is there anything at all?

Read Wisdom 13:1–5 from an NRSV-CE Bible. Wisdom is part of the Catholic Old Testament. Notice the passage’s movement from created beauty and power to their source; do not use it to insult classmates who question the argument.

Contingent means able not to exist or dependent for its existence. Necessary means not dependent in that way. The simplified sequence introduces a philosophical argument; students should ask whether dependent reality requires an ultimate explanation.

Aquinas’s Five Ways include change, efficient causation, contingency, degrees of perfection and natural directedness. They should not all be reduced to “everything that begins has a cause.” His arguments do not require a scientific demonstration of a first moment.

A beginning-based argument and a contingency argument are related but different. Further argument is required to connect an ultimate source with the full Christian understanding of God; this diagram alone does not establish the Trinity.

[Aquinas: Summa I, question 2](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/1002.htm)

[Aquinas: Summa I, question 46](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/1046.htm)


### 7. God gives being and sustains it

Explain creatio ex nihilo as creation without pre-existing material, not a physical transition from a scientific substance called nothing. A physical vacuum is not philosophical nothingness.

Avoid “everything has a cause, except God.” The arguments concern changing, caused or contingent reality; God is proposed as an uncaused, necessary source. Students may still question whether that conclusion follows.

Catholic faith teaches a beginning of creation; Aquinas distinguished that revealed teaching from what reason alone could demonstrate about a beginning in time. Even a hypothetical eternal created world would still depend on God.

Optional readings Hebrews 11:3 and Acts 17:28 deepen the theological claim. Do not read them as replacements for cosmological investigation.

[CCC 295–301](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_4_the_creator.index.html)


### 8. A universe that can support life

Fine-tuning concerns sensitivity to physical parameters under specified models. Avoid saying that every small change in every constant necessarily destroys all possible life. Often the discussion concerns life resembling ours.

An advocate argues that intentional design best explains life-permitting order. Questions include which parameters may vary, whether they vary independently, and what probability distribution is justified. Do not reproduce the note’s confident numerical-odds implication without a model.

Discuss other proposed explanations fairly: deeper physical necessity, selection effects in a possible multiverse, or treating some features as unexplained facts. These proposals have their own assumptions and unresolved questions.

Fine-tuning and biological adaptation are different topics. Distinguish a life-permitting physical environment from how organisms change within that environment.

[Barnes: Fine-tuning of the universe for life](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.07783)

[Barnes: probability and model testing](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.03965)


### 9. Order, beauty and purpose

Read Psalm 19:1–4. This poetic praise is not a technical design argument, but it gives a biblical expression of perceiving creation as a sign of God.

Distinguish the philosophical question about nature’s directedness and order from the claim that every complicated structure must have been assembled directly. Aquinas’s Fifth Way is not simply a modern DNA argument.

Augustine invites reflection on changing beauty and its source. Paraphrase that insight rather than reproducing an unverified long quotation from the note.

Ask students to offer a natural example and explain the inference, not just report that it is impressive. Wonder starts inquiry; the strength of an argument depends on its reasons.

[Aquinas: the Five Ways](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/1002.htm)


### 10. Studying life, asking about its source

The note compares DNA with a code. Explain that this is a useful analogy for information and function; it does not by itself prove a human-like author or demonstrate that evolution cannot produce biological complexity.

Evolution involves genetic variation and processes including natural selection; selection is not simply random assembly. The origin of the first life and the later evolution of life are distinct scientific questions.

Catholic belief in creation is compatible with serious study of evolution. The Church also affirms that the spiritual soul is not reducible to a material process; later lessons distinguish philosophical and theological claims from biological mechanisms.

Avoid a God-of-the-gaps argument: an unexplained mechanism today is not proof of direct divine intervention. A stronger philosophical question concerns the existence and intelligibility of nature even where mechanisms are well understood.

[NHGRI: Evolution](https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Evolution)

[International Theological Commission: Communion and Stewardship, 63–70](https://www.vatican.va/roman_curia/congregations/cfaith/cti_documents/rc_con_cfaith_doc_20040723_communion-stewardship_en.html)


### 11. Why ought we to do good?

Read Romans 2:14–16. The passage recognizes moral awareness among those without the written law. It must not become a claim that only religious believers can act morally.

State the argument fairly: if objective moral obligations need a transcendent ground, their reality points toward God. The grounding premise is philosophical and contested; merely observing shared moral feelings does not establish it.

Objective means more than personal or majority preference. Cultures show both common moral insights and serious disagreements. Neither universal agreement nor perfect human judgment is assumed.

God is understood as good, not as an arbitrary ruler whose commands make cruelty good. Lessons 1.11 and 1.12 will explore natural law, conscience formation and responsible action in depth.

[CCC 33](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_one/chapter_one/ii_ways_of_coming_to_know_god.index.html)


### 12. Can a non-believer act morally?

Invite pairs to respond before revealing. The note’s “without God everything would be permissible” can easily be misheard as an accusation against atheists; distinguish an ontological grounding claim from how people actually know and act.

Non-theistic moral realists offer accounts of objective moral truths without God. Other ethical approaches offer different accounts of duty and value. Students can evaluate these without pretending there is a universally accepted philosophical conclusion.

Evolutionary and social explanations may illuminate how moral capacities develop. Whether they fully explain moral truth and obligation is a further philosophical question, not something settled by dismissing humans as mere molecules.

Conscience can be mistaken and requires formation. Do not equate every feeling of guilt with an infallible message from God.


### 13. What is it like to be someone?

Explain subjective experience with hearing music or feeling pain. Correlations between brain processes and experience are important evidence; their philosophical interpretation remains debated.

The hard problem asks why and how physical processes are accompanied by experience. Materialist, dualist and other accounts disagree. An unresolved question alone does not prove a soul or God.

Catholic teaching affirms a spiritual soul and the unity of the person, not a ghost merely inhabiting a disposable body. Human reason, freedom and openness to truth are part of its account.

This introduces themes developed in 1.8 Humanity and 1.9 The Soul. Keep the present focus on why human experience enters discussion of God.


### 14. A desire for more than the moment

Read Ecclesiastes 3:9–13. The NRSV-CE wording of verse 11 speaks of a sense of past and future; do not replace it with the note’s wording from another translation. The text also stresses limits to human understanding.

Augustine’s restless-heart prayer expresses a Christian interpretation of desire. C. S. Lewis’s argument from desire compares natural hunger with a longing finite goods cannot satisfy; present this as an analogy, not a deductive guarantee.

A desire by itself does not establish that its object exists. Ask whether the analogy is persuasive and what further reasons would support it. Longing can be interpreted differently by thoughtful people.

Do not imply that enjoyment of ordinary life is unspiritual or that lack of a powerful religious feeling means someone is deficient. The reading includes joy in daily gifts.


### 15. An encounter that changes a life

Read Acts 9:1–9. Briefly locate Saul as a persecutor traveling to Damascus. Optional extension through verse 19 includes Ananias, restored sight and Baptism, connecting later with 1.6.

Distinguish Scripture’s account from a report a person brings to class. A transformative effect can matter as evidence, but positive change alone does not establish every interpretation of an experience.

Psychology and neuroscience can investigate aspects of religious experience. Their findings neither automatically prove nor disprove that God acts; specific claims still deserve evidence and discernment.

Students need not describe private experiences. Offer nature, prayer or an admired person as examples without requiring a testimony. A strong feeling is not required for faith.


### 16. We can build it. Should we use it?

This classroom case applies the note’s technology and ethics discussion. Do not assume the hypothetical product works or complies with any particular policy. Students should identify missing evidence as well as values.

Science can investigate error rates, bias, effects and alternatives. Moral reasoning asks whether uses respect persons, avoid unjust treatment and support students rather than label them permanently.

Science uses its own ethical norms and scientists can be morally thoughtful without religious belief. The point is that empirical findings alone do not determine every moral obligation.

Scientism is the philosophical claim that scientific inquiry is the only legitimate route to knowledge. Critiquing that claim is different from rejecting science. Faith, philosophy and ethical reflection can contribute to decisions.


### 17. A dialogue that can deepen understanding

Use Lemaître and Gregor Mendel, the Augustinian friar known for work on inheritance, as examples rather than implying that all scientists share a belief. The Vatican Observatory offers an institutional example of Catholic scientific work.

Do not tell a history of effortless harmony: real conflicts and errors have occurred, including the Galileo controversy. The Church’s ideal is dialogue, not a claim that every past judgment was correct.

John Paul II’s letter to George Coyne describes mutual correction between science and religion. Paraphrase its insight: scientific evidence can challenge superstition, and moral and religious reflection can challenge treating scientific power as an absolute.

Science and faith are not interchangeable. Neither a scientist’s personal belief nor a religious institution’s sponsorship establishes a scientific theory; evidence and sound reasoning remain necessary.

[John Paul II: Letter to George Coyne](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/letters/1988/documents/hf_jp-ii_let_19880601_padre-coyne.html)


### 18. From seeking a source to knowing God

Read Hebrews 1:1–3. The Son is presented as God’s definitive self-expression and sustainer of all things. The passage helps move from philosophical theism to specifically Christian revelation.

CCC 36 affirms that God can be known with certainty through created reality by natural reason; CCC 37–38 recognizes obstacles and the help of revelation. This is a statement of Catholic teaching, not a claim that every individual will find a short classroom argument conclusive.

Reason alone does not establish the full mysteries of the Trinity or Incarnation. Revelation is transmitted through Scripture and Apostolic Tradition and interpreted within the Church.

Avoid fideism, which dismisses reason, and a rationalism that refuses anything beyond what reason can establish by itself. Faith is not mere credulity; reason is not required to replace every dimension of trust.

[CCC 36–38](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_one/chapter_one/iii_the_knowledge_of_god_according_to_the_church.index.html)


### 19. One question, a developing answer

Present these as a roadmap when teaching in order. If students have already encountered the later lessons, ask them to retrieve one specific connection.

Lessons 1.2 and 1.3 are prepared for Batch 1 review. Lesson 1.4 is an existing source awaiting the later rollout batch.

Ask students to connect a premise or passage from today with one later topic. For example: moral awareness and conscience, creation and dignity, or revelation in Christ and the Paschal Mystery.


### 20. A reasoned search, an open conversation

Model 1: a contingency argument reasons from dependent beings to a necessary source; examine the demand for an ultimate explanation and whether the inference succeeds. Other arguments are acceptable if stated accurately.

Model 2: cosmic expansion is studied through physical evidence; attributing the ultimate existence of the universe to God requires philosophical reasoning. A current gap in scientific knowledge is not itself proof.

Model 3: Catholic teaching affirms that reason can know God from creation while revelation, received in faith, discloses God more fully. The Trinity is not a conclusion established by a Big Bang measurement.

For the final reflection, require a reason and an honest question rather than agreement. A student can explain Catholic teaching accurately while holding a different personal view.

Invite a concrete next step: read a source, clarify a premise, examine evidence or discuss a question respectfully. Intellectual humility and serious inquiry belong together.
